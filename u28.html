Cheat Sheet Blueprint

One page, double-sided · what to actually write

You get one page, front and back, plus the textbook and a calculator. Most people waste the page on definitions they already know. Definitions are in the open book. Put things you cannot look up quickly on the sheet: formulas, decision rules, and the shape of diagrams.

Handwrite it. The act of writing is most of the value, and the sheet you copy from someone else is worth almost nothing.

Also worth the space

  • Thumbnail sketches of the six diagrams you find hardest — tiny, just axes and curve names. Recognition beats reconstruction under time pressure.
  • The tariff welfare table (CS, PS, revenue, DWL = d + f). It is fiddly and you will not want to rederive it.
  • Named studies with one-line findings: Card (Mariel, no wage effect, elastic demand), Autor–Dorn–Hanson (China shock, concentrated and persistent), Piketty–Saez (tax data, top shares), Benzarti (VAT asymmetry), Frankel–Romer (geography instrument), Hall–Jones (social infrastructure), Heckman (early childhood returns).

Exam-day sequence

  1. Read the whole exam first. Two minutes. You will spot which long question is cheapest.
  2. Budget by points. 60 points in 60 minutes means a point a minute. A 4-point graph gets four minutes, not twelve.
  3. Draw before you write. The diagram usually generates the sentences.
  4. Label everything. Axes, curves, equilibria, shaded regions. This is free credit and the most commonly forfeited.
  5. Answer the question asked. If it says "briefly explain," two sentences. Edwards penalizes padding: less is more.
  6. Leave nothing blank. A named model and a correct direction earns partial credit almost every time.

If you only have one evening

Do Unit 25 end to end, then Units 26 and 27 under timed conditions. That is roughly three hours and it covers the highest-density scoring in the course. Then handwrite the cheat sheet above from memory, checking only what you cannot recall — the gaps you find are exactly what to review next.
