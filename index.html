How to run this packet

This is not a sheet to reread. Rereading feels productive and does almost nothing. The packet is built around one idea: you only own a concept once you can produce it from blank paper.

The loop

  1. Read the idea. Once, slowly. It is short on purpose.
  2. Cover the graph and redraw it on blank paper — axes, curve labels, equilibrium, shaded areas. Then compare.
  3. Read the moves. These are the sentence patterns that actually score points on Edwards's exams.
  4. Do the drills without looking. Write the answer out, then reveal. Tick the box only if you got the substance, not just the vibe.

Your ticks save in this browser, so the progress bar in the sidebar is real. If you can only do one thing: draw every graph in Unit 25 from memory. That is where most of the exam's points live.

What the exams actually look like

Both past exams are open-book with a one-page double-sided cheat sheet and a calculator. The midterm is 60 minutes for 60 points; the final is 90 minutes for 60 points and is cumulative, with the long questions weighted toward second-half material. Question types, in the order they cost you points:

TypeWhat it looks likeHow to beat it
Draw and label"Draw what supply and demand look like here, and add any additional curves you need."Axes labelled, every curve named, equilibrium marked with values, shaded areas named.
Short conceptual"Which curve slopes downward, and why?"Two sentences. State it, then give the mechanism. Never just restate the question.
NumericalElasticity, present value, the Keynesian cross, multipliers.Show your work on one line. Answer in the units asked for.
Applied narrativeEggs, Taylor Swift, soybeans, Big Macs — a current-events story wrapped in a model.Name the model first, then map the story onto it.

Edwards writes on the exam: "Confine your answers to the space provided. Don't fill the space with words. Less is more." He means it. A tight three-sentence answer with a correct diagram outscores a paragraph of hedging.

The four sentences that carry the whole course

1. Demand slopes down because marginal benefit falls.Every demand curve in this course is a marginal benefit curve, i.e. willingness to pay.
2. Supply slopes up because marginal cost rises.Every supply curve in this course is a marginal cost curve.
3. Efficiency is MB = MC.Any wedge between them — tax, monopoly, externality, tariff, price control — creates deadweight loss.
4. Optimizers set marginal benefit equal to marginal cost.Households: mu/P equal across goods. Firms: MR = MC. Hiring: MRPL = W. Machines: PV of MRPK = price.

If you can defend those four sentences and draw the graphs behind them, most of this course is bookkeeping.
