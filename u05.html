Price Controls

Classes 5–6 · Chapter 6 Midterm

A price ceiling is a legal maximum, binding only if set below equilibrium. A price floor is a legal minimum, binding only if set above. Berkeley's own rent control, in force until 1998 and since 1999 applying as "stabilization" only after a lease starts, is the running example.

A binding ceiling creates a shortage: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. The good then has to be rationed some way other than price — queuing, lotteries, government rationing as with COVID vaccines in 2021, or connections.

The second loss nobody mentions

The diagram quietly assumes the units that do get traded go to the buyers who value them most. With a shortage there is no mechanism enforcing that. So on top of the deadweight loss triangle there is misallocation: an elderly tenant may hold a rent-controlled apartment that a wealthier student would pay far more for and cannot get. Naming misallocation separately from deadweight loss is worth real credit.

The honest case for rent control

Edwards does not leave it at "controls are bad":

  • It does benefit the consumers who successfully get a unit.
  • It protects tenants from price swings driven by demand and supply shocks — insurance the model does not price.
  • The main alternative is subsidized public housing, where the government controls both price and quantity, widely used across the world and not subject to the same shortage mechanism.

Shortages and queues also appear without any government control, because prices are sticky: Saturday-night restaurants, street parking on game days, and Uber surge pricing that customers punish firms for using. In recessions the labor market queues too — unemployed workers cannot instantly bid wages down.

The moves
  1. State whether the control binds. A ceiling above equilibrium does nothing at all.
  2. Mark quantity traded at the short side — QS under a ceiling, QD under a floor.
  3. Label the shortage or surplus as a horizontal gap, not a vertical one.
  4. Say "deadweight loss plus possible misallocation among consumers."
Traps
  • Reading quantity off the demand curve under a ceiling. Nothing forces suppliers to produce more than QS.
  • Asserting consumer surplus falls. It can rise — the transfer from producers may exceed the triangle lost.
  • Drawing a ceiling above the equilibrium price and then finding a shortage.
Drills · Unit 5
graphDraw a binding price ceiling. Label the shortage, the new consumer and producer surplus, and the deadweight loss. Does consumer surplus rise or fall?
Answer

Horizontal line at PC below P₁. Quantity traded is QS, read off supply. The shortage is the horizontal gap QD − QS at that price.

Consumer surplus is the area under demand and above PC out to QS; producer surplus is the small triangle under PC and above supply; the deadweight loss is the triangle between supply and demand from QS to Q₁.

Consumer surplus is ambiguous. Consumers gain a rectangle transferred from producers but lose a triangle from units no longer traded. Which dominates depends on the elasticities. Total surplus unambiguously falls.

conceptWhy is the true welfare cost of a price ceiling larger than the deadweight loss triangle?
Answer

Because of misallocation. The triangle assumes the units still traded go to the consumers with the highest willingness to pay, but in a shortage nothing enforces that — allocation happens by queuing, luck or connections.

The standard example: an elderly tenant holds a rent-controlled apartment while a student who would pay far more cannot find one. That lost value sits on top of the triangle.

conceptGive two arguments for rent control that the simple diagram leaves out, and name the main policy alternative.
Answer
  • It genuinely benefits tenants who secure a unit, which is a real distributional gain the efficiency calculation ignores.
  • It provides insurance against price volatility from demand and supply shocks — valuable to risk-averse households and absent from the static model.

The alternative is subsidized public housing, where government sets both price and quantity, so supply does not contract in response to the controlled price. It is used widely around the world.

conceptGive two examples of shortages and queuing in markets with no price controls at all.
Answer

Price rigidity does it on its own. Restaurants on a Saturday night and street parking during a football game are both in excess demand at the posted price, and neither adjusts in real time — sellers who do adjust, like Uber's surge pricing, get punished by customers for it.

The labor market queues the same way: in a recession the unemployed cannot instantly bid wages down, and in a boom employers cannot bid wages up for new hires only.
