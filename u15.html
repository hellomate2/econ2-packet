International Trade

Class 15 · Chapter 15 Final

Unit 2's comparative advantage, now between countries. A country tends to have a comparative advantage in goods that use inputs it holds in abundance — factor abundance. Minerals drove early US industrialization; climate and soil decide where coffee grows; capital and skilled labor shape the US today; abundant less-skilled labor gives many developing countries an advantage in low-tech manufacturing. Some advantage is dynamic — acquired by getting an early start rather than inherited.

The two-country example

Output per worker-daySoybeans (tons)Washing machinesOpp. cost of 1 ton soyOpp. cost of 1 machine
U.S.221 machine1 ton
China122 machines½ ton

The US is the low-opportunity-cost soybean producer; China is the low-opportunity-cost washing machine producer. The US PPC has slope −1, China's has slope −2.

Terms of trade

The terms of trade is the world relative price of your exports — here, machines per ton of soybeans. If soybeans cost $400 and machines $300 on world markets, the terms of trade are 400/300 = 1⅓ machines per ton.

For both countries to trade, the terms of trade must lie between the two opportunity costs — here between 1 and 2 machines per ton. Outside that range, one country does better producing for itself. If soybeans were only $200 against $300, giving ⅔ machines per ton, China would happily buy but the US would refuse to sell, and excess demand would bid the relative price of soybeans back up.

With a linear PPC the tangent line touches at a corner, which is why the simple model gives complete specialization. With a realistic bowed-out PPC the tangency is interior, which is why countries specialize incompletely — the honest version.

Does trade actually help? The evidence chain

Sachs and Warner compared always-open with always-closed economies from 1966–90 and found much faster growth among the open ones. The obvious objection is reverse causation — maybe getting rich makes you trade more — plus omitted variables, since countries that adopt free trade tend to adopt other growth-friendly policies too.

Frankel and Romer answer this with an instrument: they predict trade from geography — country size, distance from partners, whether landlocked — which plausibly affects income only through trade. The geography-predicted component of trade is still positively associated with income per worker. Dollar and Kraay add that developing-country "globalisers" that opened from the late 1970s grew substantially faster afterwards.

The moves
  1. Build the opportunity cost table. Both goods, both countries, four numbers.
  2. Assign specialization to the low-opportunity-cost producer of each good.
  3. State the range the terms of trade must fall in, and why outside it one country refuses.
  4. For the graph: PPC, then CPC as a line with slope = −terms of trade tangent to it, then a production point and a consumption point.
Traps
  • Terms of trade upside down. It is the price of your exports relative to your imports.
  • Drawing the CPC inside the PPC. If it is not outside, there are no gains.
  • Claiming complete specialization with a bowed PPC. That is the linear case only.
Drills · Unit 15
calcUS: 2 tons soybeans or 2 washing machines per worker-day. China: 1 ton or 2 machines. Who exports what, and what range must the terms of trade fall in?
Answer

US: 1 ton of soybeans costs 1 machine. China: 1 ton costs 2 machines. The US is the low-opportunity-cost soybean producer and exports soybeans; China exports washing machines.

The terms of trade must lie between 1 and 2 machines per ton of soybeans. Below 1 the US would rather make its own machines; above 2 China would rather grow its own soybeans. A price of 1⅓ — soybeans $400, machines $300 — sits comfortably inside.

graphDraw a bowed-out PPC with a consumption possibilities curve. Show the production point, the consumption point, exports and imports, and where the gains are.
Answer

Bowed-out PPC. The CPC is a straight line with slope equal to minus the terms of trade, drawn tangent to the PPC.

Point A is the tangency — where the country produces, because that bundle has the highest value at world prices. Point B is somewhere else along the CPC — where it consumes after trading.

The horizontal gap between A and B is exports of the good on that axis; the vertical gap is imports of the other. The gains from trade are visible as the CPC lying outside the PPC, so the country consumes a bundle it could not possibly have produced.

conceptSachs and Warner found open economies grow faster. Give two reasons not to read that as causal, and explain how Frankel and Romer address it.
Answer
  • Reverse causation: being rich may cause you to trade more, rather than the other way round.
  • Omitted variables: countries adopting free trade tend to adopt other growth-friendly policies at the same time, so trade may just be a marker.

Frankel and Romer use geography as an instrument: they predict how much a country trades from size, distance from trading partners and whether it is landlocked. Geography is not caused by income and plausibly affects income only through trade. The geography-predicted component of trade is still positively associated with income per worker, which is much harder to explain away.

conceptWhy does the simple linear-PPC model predict complete specialization, and why is that unrealistic?
Answer

With a linear PPC, opportunity cost is constant, so a line whose slope equals minus the terms of trade touches the PPC at one of the corners — the country pours everything into one good.

Real PPCs are bowed out because opportunity cost rises as you specialize. The CPC is then tangent at an interior point, so the country produces some of both goods. That is why countries specialize incompletely in practice.
