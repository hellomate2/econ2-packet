Scarcity, Choice & Opportunity Cost

Class 2 · Chapter 1 Midterm + Final

Economics starts from one fact: constraints are real. People, firms and countries cannot have everything, so every choice gives something up. The value of what you give up is the opportunity cost, and it is the single most tested idea in the first half of this course.

Two principles run underneath everything:

  • The scarcity principle. Having more of something means having less of something else.
  • The cost–benefit principle. Take an action if and only if its benefits outweigh its costs.

The trap is that opportunity cost is often not the price tag. Sometimes it is — the opportunity cost of a burrito is the $12 you hand over. But the exam loves the cases where it is not:

  • College. Tuition and fees, plus forgone earnings. Edwards's own answer key notes average earnings around $60,000, which makes forgone wages the bigger cost at a public university. An answer that lists only tuition does not get full credit.
  • Painting your own house. Paint and brushes, plus the value of your time.
  • Using a concert ticket whose market price has changed. The cost is what you could sell it for now, not what you paid. What you paid is sunk.

The production possibilities curve

The PPC is the first model in the course: all combinations of two goods an economy can produce using all its labor, capital and technology. Its shape encodes three things at once.

The slope of the PPC is minus the opportunity cost of the good on the horizontal axis. Write that on your cheat sheet. If the horizontal axis is coconuts and the slope is −3, then one more coconut costs three fish.

A bowed-out (concave) PPC means rising opportunity cost. As you push into one good, you burn through your best-suited resources and start reallocating resources that were better at the other thing. Unit 2 shows exactly where that curvature comes from.

Shifts come from more labor (immigration, population growth), more capital, or better technology. An economy that chooses more investment goods today gets a bigger PPC tomorrow — that is the consumption-versus-investment tradeoff, and roughly 75% of US production is consumption, 25% gross investment.

The moves
  1. Asked for the cost of anything: name the out-of-pocket cost and the forgone alternative. The second half is where the credit is.
  2. Asked about a PPC slope: convert it into a sentence — "one more X costs Y units of Z."
  3. Asked whether something shifts the PPC or moves you along it: only labor, capital and technology shift it. Unemployment, recessions and bad choices move you inside it.
Traps
  • Treating sunk costs as costs. What you already paid for the ticket is irrelevant.
  • Forgetting forgone earnings in the college question. It has appeared on a real exam.
  • Drawing a recession as an inward shift of the PPC. It is an interior point.
Drills · Unit 1
examList and briefly discuss the most important costs of attending UC Berkeley.
Answer

Two dominate. Tuition and fees — the money actually paid to the Regents. And forgone earnings — the wages you could have earned working full time instead of enrolling. With average annual earnings near $60,000, forgone earnings are likely the larger cost at a public university like Berkeley, though not at an expensive private one.

Room and board and books are acceptable secondary answers, but an answer without forgone earnings does not earn full credit. Room and board is also partly not a true cost of college, since you would have to eat and live somewhere anyway.

conceptYou bought a concert ticket for $80. By showtime, identical tickets resell for $300. What is the opportunity cost of going?
Answer

$300 plus the value of your time — the current market price, not the $80 you paid. The $80 is sunk and identical whether you attend or not. If you would not pay $300 for the experience today, going is the wrong call, however much it stings.

graphDraw a PPC with consumption goods on the vertical axis and investment goods on the horizontal. Mark an efficient point, an unattainable point, and a recession. Then show what the future PPC looks like if the economy picks a high-investment point today.
Answer

Bowed-out curve from the C-intercept to the I-intercept. An efficient point sits on the curve; an unattainable point sits beyond it; a recession sits strictly inside it.

A high-investment point today means more machines, buildings and R&D tomorrow, so draw a second PPC further out for the future. A high-consumption choice today gives a future PPC that is much closer in. This is the clean way to see why investment matters for growth — and it reappears in Unit 19.

conceptWhy is a country's PPC usually bowed out rather than a straight line?
Answer

Because resources are not equally good at both goods. Producing the first units of a good uses the resources best suited to it, at low opportunity cost. Pushing further means pulling in resources that were relatively better at the other good, so the opportunity cost rises as you produce more — and rising opportunity cost is a concave curve. Unit 2 shows this concretely with two workers.
