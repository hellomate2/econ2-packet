Long-Run Growth

Class 18 · Chapter 19 Final

Robert Lucas: "Once you start thinking about growth, it's hard to think about anything else." The reason is compounding.

Two countries start equal; one grows 1 percentage point faster per year. After 1 year it is 1% ahead. After 2 years, 2.01%. After 70 years it is twice as rich. After two centuries, more than seven times. Small differences in growth rates dominate everything else about long-run living standards.

The framework

From Unit 17: Y*/POP = (N*/POP) × (Y*/N*), and productivity depends on capital per worker and technology. Ask which piece explains the differences we see.

SourceAcross countriesOver time
N*/POPNot very important — it does not vary muchMattered in some periods (women's labor force participation) but cannot rise forever, and diminishing returns blunt it
K*/N*Very important — varies enormouslySomewhat important; diminishing returns mean doubling capital per worker less than doubles output per worker
TechnologyVery important — roughly half of cross-country differences with capitalCrucial — perhaps two-thirds of growth, by elimination

The sharpest finding: the variation in "technology" that matters across countries is not knowledge, it is institutions. Knowledge is broadly available; what differs is whether a society can use it. Three features matter:

  • A market-based system for allocating resources.
  • Government protection of property from others.
  • Protection of property from the government — corruption, theft, arbitrary taxation.

That third item is the one people forget, and the final exam tests it directly as "social infrastructure": rule of law, property rights, and protection from government. Hall and Jones showed average labor productivity tracks social infrastructure closely.

Growth in the labor market diagram

Edwards's preferred framing for ECON 2. Only permanent outward shifts in labor demand raise wages and employment in the long run, and labor demand is MRPL = P × MPL. So growth comes from anything that raises the price of what workers make (trade, better terms of trade) or raises their marginal product (capital, technology). Shifts big enough can also sustain population growth — labor supply shifting out — while wages keep rising.

Where technological progress comes from

New production techniques (electric motor, tractor, assembly line, modern accounting), new products (Nordhaus's list runs from aeronautics and air-conditioning through penicillin, the transistor and the digital computer), and better institutions such as opening to trade.

Does the free market supply the socially optimal amount of invention? Almost certainly not — inventions have large positive externalities, especially basic science, so a private inventor captures only part of the social benefit. That is a Unit 11 argument applied to ideas, and it justifies subsidizing R&D, funding basic research directly, strengthening property rights in inventions, and expanding education. The 2025 Nobel to Mokyr, Aghion and Howitt sits here: Mokyr on how scientific innovation must connect to practical and commercial knowledge and social acceptance of change, Aghion and Howitt modelling growth as creative destruction, where profitable innovation is driven by temporary patent monopolies.

The moves
  1. Decompose into N*/POP, K*/N*, and T. Then say which piece the question is really about.
  2. If asked why poor countries stay poor, lead with institutions, not knowledge.
  3. Use the compounding numbers — 1 point faster doubles you in 70 years.
  4. If asked to draw growth, use the labor market with labor demand shifting out.
Traps
  • Explaining cross-country income gaps with capital alone. Institutions do about half.
  • Thinking poor countries lack knowledge. They lack the institutions to deploy it.
  • Forgetting protection from government. It is a third of the social infrastructure answer.
Drills · Unit 19
examWhich is an important aspect of "social infrastructure"? (a) rule of law, (b) property rights, (c) protection from government, (d) all of the above.
Answer

(d) All of the above.

Social infrastructure is the set of basic building blocks that let markets function: the rule of law, property rights, and — the one people leave out — protection of property from the government itself, meaning from corruption, expropriation, theft and arbitrary taxation. Hall and Jones showed average labor productivity tracks it closely across countries.

calcCountry A grows 1 percentage point faster than B from the same starting point. How much richer after 70 years? After 200?
Answer

After 70 years: 1.0170 ≈ 2, so twice as rich. After 200 years: 1.01200 ≈ 7.3, so more than seven times.

(After 1 year, 1% richer; after 2, 1.01² = 1.0201, so 2.01% richer.) The lesson is that small, sustained differences in growth rates swamp almost everything else about long-run living standards — which is why Lucas said that once you think about growth it is hard to think about anything else.

conceptWhich contributes most to growth in potential output per person over time, and how do we know?
Answer

Technology — perhaps two-thirds of growth. The argument is by elimination.

  • N*/POP does not change much, cannot rise indefinitely, and when it rises, capital per worker falls, so output per person rises less than proportionally.
  • K*/N* matters somewhat — human capital has risen a lot — but diminishing returns mean doubling capital per worker less than doubles output per worker, and observed increases are not large enough to account for observed growth.

What is left is technology, broadly defined to include institutions.

graphUse a labor market diagram to show the escape from the Malthusian trap around 1800.
Answer

Before 1800: labor demand is essentially static. Clark's English data from 1250 to about 1640 scatter along a single downward-sloping labor demand curve — when population rose, labor supply shifted out along a fixed demand curve and real wages fell. That is the Malthusian trap: more people, lower living standards.

After 1800: labor demand begins shifting out persistently, driven by capital accumulation and technology raising MPL. Now labor supply can shift out with population growth and wages still rise. Clark's scatter stops tracing a curve and becomes an arrow heading up and to the right.

conceptDoes the free market produce the socially optimal amount of invention?
Answer

Almost certainly not. Inventions carry large positive externalities — the inventor captures only part of the social benefit while others build on the idea. By Unit 11's logic, the market therefore underproduces invention.

This is especially true of basic science, where the payoff is distant, diffuse and unpatentable. Hence the policy list: subsidize R&D, fund basic research directly, make property rights in inventions secure, and expand education.
