Comparative Advantage

Classes 2–3 · Chapter 2 Midterm + Final

This unit has appeared on both past exams — as a short question on the midterm and as a multiple choice on the final. Learn the distinction cold.

  • Absolute advantage: you can produce more of a good per hour than someone else.
  • Comparative advantage: you give up less of the other good to produce it. You are the low-opportunity-cost producer.

Only comparative advantage determines who should specialize. The final exam asks this directly: the principle of comparative advantage implies not that I should specialize in what I do best, nor in everything I do better than others, but in the task where my opportunity cost is lowest.

The island, worked completely

Bill and Chris live on an island. In one hour, Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut. Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts. Each works 10 hours a day. Chris has an absolute advantage in both.

Bill: 1 coconut costs 1 fish. Chris: 1 coconut costs 4 fish.Bill is the low-opportunity-cost coconut producer, so Bill has the comparative advantage in coconuts — despite being worse at everything.

Flip it to check: a fish costs Bill 1 coconut but costs Chris only ¼ coconut. Chris is the low-cost fish producer. Comparative advantage in one good always implies the other party has it in the other good.

The shaded gap between the two frontiers is the gains from specialization, and its size depends on how different the opportunity costs are. Identical opportunity costs means no gains at all.

The technology twist

A past midterm asked: a new fishhook doubles both men's fishing productivity. Does the specialization strategy change?

No. Bill now catches 2 fish or 1 coconut; Chris catches 16 fish or 2 coconuts. A coconut costs Bill 2 fish and Chris 8 fish. The numbers moved, the ranking did not. Chris still has the comparative advantage in fish, Bill in coconuts. This is the general lesson: a proportional productivity change in one good for everyone leaves comparative advantage untouched.

Where it shows up later

Comparative advantage is also the engine of Units 15 and 16. Countries tend to have a comparative advantage in goods that use the inputs they hold in abundance — factor abundance. Climate for coffee, minerals for early US industry, capital and skilled labor for the US today, less-skilled labor for many developing economies. Some of it is acquired rather than inherent, which is dynamic comparative advantage.

The California rice case study is the cleanest test of the idea. California grows a lot of rice, but rice needs enormous water, and California's water has high-value alternative uses — nuts, vegetables, urban growth. Arkansas gives up far less. California is almost certainly not the low-opportunity-cost rice producer, and it specializes anyway for historical reasons dating to the Gold Rush. History can override opportunity cost.

The moves
  1. Build the opportunity-cost table first. Always. "For A, one X costs ___ Y."
  2. Compare opportunity costs, never outputs. Whoever gives up less specializes.
  3. Say the magic sentence: "A's opportunity cost of producing X is lower than B's, so A specializes in X."
  4. For gains from trade, the terms of trade must land between the two opportunity costs, or one side refuses.
Traps
  • Concluding the more productive person should do everything. That is absolute advantage, and it is the wrong answer.
  • Getting the fraction upside down. A coconut costing 4 fish means a fish costs ¼ coconut.
  • Thinking a uniform productivity boost changes who specializes. It does not.
Drills · Unit 2
examChris is much more efficient than Bill at both activities but comparatively better at fishing. Provided both want some fish and some coconuts, who specializes in what, and why?
Answer

Chris specializes in fish, Bill in coconuts. Chris's opportunity cost of gathering a coconut is 4 fish, while Bill's is only 1 fish. Equivalently, Bill's opportunity cost of fishing is higher than Chris's. Absolute advantage in both goods is irrelevant to the answer.

examA new fishhook doubles both men's fishing productivity. What changes about optimal specialization?
Answer

Nothing qualitatively. Bill now gets 2 fish or 1 coconut per hour; Chris gets 16 fish or 2 coconuts. A coconut costs Bill 2 fish and Chris 8 fish, so Chris retains the comparative advantage in fish and Bill in coconuts.

Worth one extra sentence for credit: both are richer, and if they get tired of fish they may want Chris to fish somewhat less — but Bill still always specializes in coconuts.

calcTom produces 24 lbs of nuts or 12 lbs of coffee per day. Krishna produces 12 lbs of nuts or 24 lbs of coffee. Who specializes where? If they each specialize fully and split output 50-50, what does each consume, and is that better than going it alone?
Answer

Tom: 1 lb coffee costs 2 lbs nuts. Krishna: 1 lb coffee costs ½ lb nuts. Krishna specializes in coffee, Tom in nuts.

Full specialization gives 24 lbs nuts and 24 lbs coffee. Split evenly, each consumes (12 nuts, 12 coffee).

Alone, Tom's frontier running from 24 nuts to 12 coffee puts (12, 12) strictly beyond his reach — at 12 nuts he could only manage 6 coffee. Same for Krishna by symmetry. Both end up outside their own PPC, which is exactly what gains from trade look like.

conceptDoes it make economic sense for California to grow rice?
Answer

Probably not on opportunity-cost grounds. Rice is extremely water-intensive, and California's scarce water has high-value alternative uses — nuts, vegetables, and urban and industrial growth. States like Arkansas give up much lower-value crops and are not constraining city growth to do it. So California is almost surely not the low-opportunity-cost rice producer.

It specializes anyway for historical reasons — Gold Rush-era settlement and demand from Chinese immigrant workers. The lesson is that observed specialization does not prove comparative advantage.

conceptName three real limits on specialization.
Answer
  • Individual: extremely narrow tasks are psychologically numbing — assembly-line work.
  • National security and supply chains: trade wars, food sufficiency, military production.
  • Infant industries that may need early protection to ever become competitive.
  • Regulatory arbitrage: environmental and labor protections differ across countries, so specialization can just relocate harm.
