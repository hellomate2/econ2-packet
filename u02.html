Comparative Advantage

Classes 2–3 · Chapter 2 Midterm + Final
If you remember one thing

Specialization follows comparative advantage, never absolute advantage: whoever gives up less of the other good does the job — even if worse at everything.

The distinction

Absolute advantage
You produce more per hour. Irrelevant to who specializes.
Comparative advantage
You give up less of the other good — the low-opportunity-cost producer. This decides everything.
Exam wording, Fall 2025 final
Keyed answer: "the task I do best" — best among my own activities = lowest opportunity cost. The wrong option, "everything I do better than others," is absolute advantage.

Bill and Chris

Each works 10 hours a day; Chris has an absolute advantage in both.

ProducerPer hour1 coconut costs1 fish costsSpecializes in
Bill1 fish or 1 coconut1 fish1 coconutCoconuts
Chris8 fish or 2 coconuts4 fish¼ coconutFish

Comparative advantage in one good implies the other party has it in the other; gains exist only because costs differ.

Technology twist

Fishhook doubles fishing → Bill 2 fish or 1 coconut; Chris 16 fish or 2 coconuts A coconut now costs Bill 2 fish, Chris 8 fish. Numbers moved, ranking did not: a proportional productivity change in one good for everyone leaves comparative advantage untouched.

Countries and rice

Factor abundance
Advantage in goods using inputs you hold in abundance: climate for coffee, minerals for early US industry, capital and skilled labor for the US today, less-skilled labor for developing economies (Units 15–16).
Dynamic comparative advantage
Advantage acquired rather than inherent.
California rice
Rice needs enormous water; California's has high-value alternatives — nuts, vegetables, urban growth — Arkansas's does not. California is almost certainly not the low-opportunity-cost rice producer; it specializes anyway, from Gold Rush history, which can override opportunity cost.

Rules that earn points

  1. Build the opportunity-cost table first: "for A, one X costs ___ Y."
  2. Compare opportunity costs, never outputs: "A's cost of X is lower than B's, so A specializes in X."
  3. Terms of trade must land between the two opportunity costs, or one side refuses.
  4. Limits on specialization: numbing narrow tasks, national security and supply chains, infant industries, differing environmental and labor rules.
Traps
  • Concluding the more productive person should do everything. That is absolute advantage.
  • Getting the fraction upside down. A coconut costing 4 fish means a fish costs ¼ coconut.
  • Thinking a uniform productivity boost changes who specializes.
Unit 2 quiz4 MCQ · 2 FRQ · ~12 min
MCQ 12 pts

Bill catches 1 fish or gathers 1 coconut per hour; Chris catches 8 fish or gathers 2 coconuts. Who has the comparative advantage in coconuts?

  • AChris — he gathers twice as many coconuts per hour
  • BBill — he gives up 1 fish per coconut, Chris gives up 4
  • CNeither — Chris is better at both, so he should produce both
  • DChris — he is more productive, so his time is worth more everywhere
Answer

B — Bill, at 1 fish per coconut against Chris's 4.

Comparative advantage is about what you forgo, not what you produce.

A is the trap: it compares coconut output, which is absolute advantage. Chris being better at both is exactly the setup that makes this question worth asking.

MCQ 22 pts

A new fishhook doubles both men's fishing productivity. Optimal specialization now:

  • AIs unchanged — Chris fishes, Bill gathers coconuts
  • BFlips — Bill now fishes, Chris gathers coconuts
  • CDisappears — they should split their time equally
  • DCannot be determined without knowing their preferences
Answer

A — unchanged.

A coconut now costs Bill 2 fish and Chris 8 fish. Both numbers doubled, so the ranking is identical: Chris keeps the comparative advantage in fish, Bill in coconuts.

B is the trap: a change in absolute productivity feels like it should move the assignment, but a proportional change to the same good for everyone cancels out of the ratio.

MCQ 32 pts

Comparative advantage implies which statement about how you should spend your time?

  • AI should specialize in everything I do better than other people
  • BI should specialize in the task I do best — best among my own activities
  • CI should specialize in whichever good has the highest market price
  • DI should specialize only if I have an absolute advantage in something
Answer

B — the task I do best among my own activities.

This was the keyed answer on the Fall 2025 final. "Best among my own activities" is just another way of saying lowest opportunity cost.

A is the trap, and it is the one most students pick: comparing yourself to others is absolute advantage.

MCQ 42 pts

Per day Tom produces 24 lbs of nuts or 12 lbs of coffee; Krishna produces 12 lbs of nuts or 24 lbs of coffee. They each specialize fully and split total output 50-50. Each consumes:

  • A12 lbs nuts, 12 lbs coffee
  • B24 lbs nuts, 24 lbs coffee
  • C12 lbs nuts, 6 lbs coffee
  • D18 lbs nuts, 18 lbs coffee
Answer

A — 12 nuts and 12 coffee each.

Tom specializes in nuts (1 lb coffee costs him 2 lbs nuts), Krishna in coffee (1 lb coffee costs Krishna ½ lb nuts). Total output is 24 nuts and 24 coffee; halved, each gets (12, 12) — strictly beyond either one's own PPC.

B is the trap: that is the pair's combined output, not one person's share.

FRQ 16 pts

Chris is much more efficient than Bill at both activities but comparatively better at fishing. (a) Who specializes in what, and why? (b) Draw the island's PPC with and without specialization and show the gains from specialization.

Write (a) in three sentences. For (b) draw coconuts on the horizontal axis and fish on the vertical, both frontiers, and shade the gap between them.
Model answer

(a) Chris specializes in fish, Bill in coconuts. Chris's opportunity cost of a coconut is 4 fish while Bill's is only 1 fish, so Bill is the low-opportunity-cost coconut producer. Equivalently, a fish costs Chris only ¼ coconut against Bill's 1 coconut. Chris's absolute advantage in both goods is irrelevant.

(b) Without specialization both switch hours together, giving up 9 fish for 3 coconuts — a straight line of slope −3. With specialization Bill switches out of fishing first at slope −1, then Chris at slope −4: a kinked frontier lying outside the straight one. The shaded gap between them is the gains from specialization, and it grows with the difference in opportunity costs.

What earns the points
  • Both opportunity costs stated numerically, not just asserted.
  • Saying absolute advantage does not decide the assignment.
  • The kinked frontier drawn outside the unspecialized line, with Bill's flatter segment first.
  • Naming the gap as the gains from specialization.
  • Linking the size of the gains to the difference in opportunity costs.
FRQ 24 pts

Does it make economic sense for California to grow rice?

Four or five sentences. Compare California's forgone uses with another rice state's, then explain the specialization you actually observe.
Model answer

Probably not on opportunity-cost grounds. Rice is extremely water-intensive, and California's scarce water has high-value alternative uses — nuts, vegetables, and urban and industrial growth. States like Arkansas give up much lower-value crops and are not constraining city growth to do it, so California is almost surely not the low-opportunity-cost rice producer.

It specializes anyway for historical reasons dating to the Gold Rush and demand from Chinese immigrant workers. Observed specialization does not prove comparative advantage.

What earns the points
  • Framing the answer as opportunity cost of water, not as "rice grows well there."
  • Naming specific high-value California alternatives.
  • An explicit comparison with Arkansas.
  • The historical explanation for the specialization we actually observe.
