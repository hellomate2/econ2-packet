International Macroeconomics

Class 23 · Chapter 28 Final

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. Get the vocabulary right, because the final tests it directly:

  • Appreciation — the price of the currency in foreign currency rises. A "strong" currency.
  • Depreciation — that price falls. A "weak" currency.

If the yen-dollar rate goes from ¥160 to ¥150 per dollar, a dollar buys fewer yen, so the dollar depreciated and the yen appreciated.

Think of a market for dollars:

  • Demand for dollars comes from foreigners buying American goods, services or assets — they must convert into dollars first.
  • Supply of dollars comes from Americans buying foreign goods, services or assets.

The balance of payments identity

At the equilibrium exchange rate, dollars demanded equal dollars supplied:

EX + KI = IM + KO
(EX − IM) + (KI − KO) = 0
NX = −NKIEX exports, IM imports, KI capital inflows (foreigners buying US assets), KO capital outflows. In words: if you export on net, you must be buying assets abroad. If you import on net, you must be selling assets abroad.

What determines net capital inflows: the US real interest rate (higher r makes US assets more attractive, raising NKI), foreign real interest rates (higher abroad lowers NKI), and tastes for US versus foreign assets.

Combine: a rise in r raises NKI, and since NX = −NKI, a rise in r lowers NX. That completes the claim from Unit 20 that C, I and NX all fall when r rises.

The long-run real interest rate

With NX* = 0, Y* = C* + I* + G rearranges to S* = I*, and r* equilibrates them. Opening the economy:

S* + NKI* = I*r* is the real rate that makes domestic saving plus net capital inflows equal domestic investment. Both S and NKI rise with r.

This is the deep explanation of the US trade deficit. If a country's investment exceeds its saving, it must import capital and run a trade deficit. The US has looked like this since the 1960s. China's surpluses reflect its very high domestic saving.

Three wrong explanations of the trade deficit

Edwards lists the popular theories and refutes them with one diagram: that US goods are low quality, that Americans have strong tastes for foreign goods, and that foreign countries are protectionist. All three predict a weak dollar, not a large trade deficit. If Americans wanted foreign goods more and foreigners wanted American goods less, supply of dollars would rise and demand would fall, so the dollar would depreciate — which would push NX back toward balance.

The explanation that survives: US assets look highly attractive — tech in the 1990s, housing in the early 2000s, perhaps AI now. That makes NKI large and positive, so NX is large and negative. On the FX diagram, foreigners buy more US assets (demand out) and Americans buy fewer foreign assets (supply in), so the dollar appreciates and NX falls.

The tariff application

Long-lasting tariffs on many goods, with no retaliation. Short run: tariffs cut US purchases of foreign goods, so the supply of dollars falls and the dollar appreciates. At a given exchange rate imports would fall and NX rise — but appreciation reduces exports and raises imports, pushing the other way.

The net answer comes from NX = −NKI. If nothing happens to net capital inflows, nothing happens to NX. The long-run saving–investment diagram confirms it: tariffs do not move S, I or NKI, so r* and NX* are unchanged.

How seriously to take it: all models are approximations, and the effect is unlikely to be literally zero. But there is a clear force working against the tariff's direct effect — the appreciating dollar — so as long as NKI does not respond much to the exchange rate, which is realistic, the impact on NX is small.

The moves
  1. Decide who needs whose currency. Foreigners buying US ⇒ demand for dollars. Americans buying abroad ⇒ supply of dollars.
  2. Interest rate changes usually shift both curves the same direction on price. Say so — it strengthens the answer.
  3. For any net exports question, go through NX = −NKI. Figure out NKI first.
  4. For long-run r*, draw S + NKI against I.
Traps
  • Getting appreciation backwards. ¥160 → ¥150 per dollar is a weaker dollar.
  • Explaining the trade deficit with tastes or quality. Those predict a weak dollar.
  • Concluding tariffs shrink the trade deficit without checking NKI.
Drills · Unit 24
examIf the yen-dollar rate falls from ¥160 to ¥150 per US dollar, what happened?
Answer

The yen appreciated and the dollar depreciated.

A dollar now buys only ¥150 instead of ¥160, so the dollar is worth less in yen — depreciation. Equivalently, a yen now costs more dollars than before, so the yen appreciated. Read the quote carefully: this one is yen per dollar, so a falling number means a weaker dollar.

graphThe US real interest rate rises. Show the effect in the market for dollars and explain the consequence for net exports.
Answer

Demand for dollars shifts out: Japanese and other foreign savers want US bonds instead of their own, and must buy dollars to do it.

Supply of dollars shifts in: American savers also now prefer US bonds, so they convert fewer dollars into foreign currency.

Both effects raise the price of the dollar, so the dollar appreciates unambiguously — quantity traded is ambiguous, but the exchange rate is not.

Consequence: a stronger dollar makes US goods expensive abroad and foreign goods cheap at home, so exports fall and imports rise — NX falls. This is the channel that completes the claim that C, I and NX all decline when r rises.

conceptDerive NX = −NKI and say what it means in words.
Answer

At the equilibrium exchange rate, dollars demanded equal dollars supplied:

EX + KI = IM + KO

Rearranging: (EX − IM) + (KI − KO) = 0, so NX + NKI = 0, i.e. NX = −NKI.

In words: if you export on net, you are accumulating claims on foreigners — buying assets abroad. If you import on net, you must be selling assets abroad to pay for it. A trade deficit is financed by a capital account surplus, necessarily and by accounting.

examThree popular explanations of the US trade deficit: low-quality US goods, American tastes for foreign goods, foreign protectionism. What is wrong with all three, and what actually explains it?
Answer

All three predict a weak dollar, not a large trade deficit. If US goods were poor and Americans craved foreign ones, demand for dollars would fall and supply would rise, so the dollar would depreciate — and a cheaper dollar pushes net exports back toward balance. They cannot explain a persistent deficit alongside a strong dollar.

What does explain it: since NX* = −NKI*, a persistently large negative NX requires a persistently large positive NKI. And US assets look highly attractive to global investors — tech in the 1990s, housing in the early 2000s, perhaps AI now.

On the diagram: foreigners buy more US assets (demand for dollars out) and Americans buy fewer foreign assets (supply in), so the dollar appreciates, and the strong dollar is what pushes NX negative. Strong dollar and trade deficit are the same phenomenon.

examThe US raises tariffs on many goods and nobody retaliates. What happens to net exports in the short run and the long run?
Answer

Short run: tariffs cut US purchases of foreign goods, so Americans supply fewer dollars to the FX market — supply shifts in and the dollar appreciates.

Two opposing forces on NX: at a given exchange rate imports fall, raising NX; but the appreciation reduces exports and raises imports, lowering NX.

The resolution is NX = −NKI. Tariffs do not change the attractiveness of US assets or relative interest rates, so NKI does not change — and therefore NX does not change, in the short run or the long run. The long-run S + NKI = I diagram confirms it: nothing moves, so r* and NX* are unchanged.

How seriously to take this: all models are approximations, so the effect is unlikely to be literally zero. But there is a genuine offsetting force — the appreciating dollar — and as long as NKI does not respond much to the exchange rate, which is realistic, the impact on NX is small.

conceptWhy would a rise in the Bank of Japan's target rate threaten US markets?
Answer

Because international investors chase higher real interest rates. A rise in Japanese real rates pulls capital into Japan, which means reduced net capital inflows to the US.

Less foreign demand for US assets means lower US asset prices and higher US interest rates — and by Unit 14, higher rates lower the present value of every future payment, so bond and stock prices fall.

Why the BOJ would raise rates in the first place: to reduce inflation by cutting PAE, through both lower investment spending (Unit 22) and an appreciated yen reducing Japanese net exports (this unit).
