Inflation & the Return to Potential

Class 22 · Chapter 27 Final

This unit closes the loop: what drags output back to potential, and what determines inflation in the long run.

The output gap is Y − Y*. When it is negative, unemployment sits above its long-run level. Two facts run the whole chapter:

  • In the short run inflation barely moves — nominal rigidity, or inflation inertia, from limited information, menu costs and long-term contracts.
  • Over time inflation responds to the output gap. Y > Y* ⇒ inflation gradually rises. Y < Y* ⇒ it gradually falls. Y = Y* ⇒ it holds steady.

The mechanism: with Y above Y*, firms are operating beyond comfortable capacity, so as contracts expire and menus wear out they raise prices by more than past inflation. Many firms doing that is rising inflation. At Y = Y*, firms simply keep up with expected inflation, and past inflation is a crucial determinant of expectations.

There is also the inflation shock: a sudden jump in a small set of prices, classically oil, unrelated to the output gap. It changes inflation immediately — 1973–74 being the textbook case.

The reaction function

The Fed raises r when inflation rises and lowers it when inflation falls. That relationship is its reaction function, and Edwards writes an example:

r − r̄ = 0.5 (π − π̄)With a target π̄ = 2% and a normal real rate r̄ = 3%: at π = 3%, r goes to 3.5% and i = 6.5%; at π = 5%, r goes to 4.5% and i = 9.5%; at π = 2%, r stays 3% and i = 5%; at π = 1%, r falls to 2.5% and i = 3.5%.

How the economy self-corrects

Suppose Y₁ < Y*. Inflation starts to fall. Following its reaction function, the Fed lowers r. Lower r raises C and IP, so PAE shifts up and Y rises. As long as Y ≠ Y*, inflation keeps changing, so the Fed keeps moving r, so Y keeps moving — until Y = Y*. Long-run equilibrium is where the PAE line crosses the 45° line exactly at Y*.

Disinflation — the exam's favorite sequence

Start in long-run equilibrium with inflation steady but too high. The Fed decides to lower it, so it shifts its reaction function up — tighter at every inflation rate.

  1. Short run: inflation does not change (nominal rigidity), but r jumps to r₂. PAE shifts down. Y falls by more than the shift, because of the multiplier. A recession, deliberately caused.
  2. Adjustment: with Y < Y*, inflation starts falling. Following its new function, the Fed lowers r. PAE shifts back up, Y rises.
  3. Long run: Y returns to Y*. Inflation was falling the whole time Y was below Y*, and never rose, so inflation ends lower. The real interest rate returns to r*, which is set by saving and investment, not by the Fed.

The slides place the inflation charts for 1976–79 and 2021–22 side by side for exactly this comparison — the late-1970s disinflation is the historical case, and the post-2021 tightening is the recent one.

The moves
  1. Answer in three stages: short run, adjustment, long run. Never skip the middle.
  2. Short run: inflation is fixed; r moves; PAE shifts; Y moves by the multiplier.
  3. Adjustment: inflation responds to the output gap; the Fed responds to inflation; PAE shifts back.
  4. Long run: Y = Y*, r = r*, and inflation is whatever the reaction function implies.
Traps
  • Letting inflation move immediately. In the short run it does not — that is the whole mechanism.
  • Saying the Fed permanently lowered the real rate. In the long run r returns to r*.
  • Forgetting that disinflation costs a recession. That cost is the point of the question.

examThe Fed shifts its reaction function up to reduce long-run inflation. Walk through the short run, the adjustment, and the long run.
Answer

Short run. Inflation does not change — nominal rigidity. But the new function means a higher r at the current inflation rate, so r jumps to r₂. Higher r cuts C, IP and NX, so PAE shifts down and Y falls by more than the shift because of the multiplier. The Fed has deliberately caused a recession.

Adjustment. With Y < Y*, inflation starts to fall. Following its new reaction function, the Fed lowers r as inflation falls. That shifts PAE back up and raises Y. The process repeats until Y = Y*.

Long run. Output is back at Y*. Inflation fell throughout the period Y was below Y*, and never rose, so inflation is permanently lower. The real interest rate returns to r*, determined by saving and investment — not by the Fed.

conceptWhy does inflation rise when Y > Y*?
Answer

In the short run it does not — inflation is inertial.

Over time, though, contracts expire, menus wear out and uncertainty resolves. With Y above Y*, firms are operating beyond their comfortable capacity, so when they get the chance to reset, they want their prices higher relative to other firms' — which means raising them by more than past inflation.

Many firms doing that simultaneously is an increase in the overall inflation rate. The same forces run in reverse when Y < Y*. At Y = Y*, firms just keep up with expected inflation, and past inflation anchors those expectations.

conceptWhy can't the Fed hold the real interest rate away from r* indefinitely?
Answer

Because holding r away from r* keeps Y away from Y*, and any persistent output gap keeps moving inflation.

Hold r too low and Y stays above Y*, so inflation rises without limit. Hold r too high and Y stays below Y*, so inflation falls without limit. Neither is sustainable, and a central bank following any sensible reaction function is forced to respond to that drifting inflation — which pulls r back toward r*.

In the long run r* is determined by saving and investment (Unit 24 adds net capital inflows), not by the central bank.

conceptWhat is an inflation shock, and how does it differ from inflation driven by the output gap?
Answer

An inflation shock is a sudden jump — usually upward — in a small set of prices, classically oil, for reasons unrelated to how Y compares with Y*. The 1973–74 oil shock is the standard case.

The key difference is timing. Output-gap inflation is gradual: it only emerges as contracts expire and prices reset. An inflation shock changes inflation immediately, with no gap required.

It is also awkward for policy: the central bank faces higher inflation without the excess demand that normally accompanies it, so fighting it means causing a recession that was not otherwise warranted.
