Inflation & Price Indexes

Classes 17–18 · Chapter 18 Final

Inflation is growth in the overall price level, so you first need a price level. The standard measure is the Consumer Price Index: fix a basket of quantities from a base year, and track what that basket costs.

CPI2022 = [ Σ Pi,2022·Qi,base ] / [ Σ Pi,base·Qi,base ] × 100Note the mirror image of real GDP: for CPI you fix quantities and let prices move. For real GDP you fix prices and let quantities move.
π2022 = (CPI2022 − CPI2021) / CPI2021 × 100Negative inflation is deflation. The Fed's target is 2%.

A fixed basket goes stale — smartphones and electric cars did not exist in 1983 — so the BLS updates it, now annually. Measuring with the old basket is Laspeyres, the new basket Paasche, and a geometric average of the two is Fisher, usually applied with chaining. Edwards says the details are not on the exam, but knowing the basket changes and why is fair game. Quality improvements get netted out of price increases, and new goods are accounted for as new ways of obtaining utility.

Converting across time — do this correctly

$XB = $XA × (PB / PA)To express a Year-A amount in Year-B dollars, multiply by the ratio of price levels, new over old.

Two worked cases from lecture. Cal in-state tuition in 1985–86 was $1,296 with a CPI of 108.94; at a CPI of 300.536 that is 1296 × (300.536/108.94) = $3,575 — against roughly $14,796 actually charged, so real tuition rose enormously. A good desktop computer in 1996 cost about $4,000 with a CPI of 156.85, which is 4000 × (300.536/156.85) = $7,664 in today's dollars — against about $1,000 today, so the real price of computing collapsed.

Why care about inflation?

There is an argument for not caring: in aggregate, expenditure equals income, so if all prices and all incomes rise together nothing real changes — like arguing about Daylight Saving Time. The reasons we care anyway:

  • Redistribution. Unexpected inflation transfers from lenders to borrowers and hurts anyone on a fixed nominal income.
  • Psychology. People experience price rises as theft and wage rises as merit, so inflation feels worse than the arithmetic suggests. It moves elections.
  • Efficiency. Volatile prices make it harder to read real signals and plan.

It can be chronically high, as in Argentina since 2002, or become hyperinflation, as in Venezuela from 2018 to 2020.

The Fisher relation — the bridge to everything

i = r + π ⟺ r = i − πStrictly it is expected inflation. The nominal rate has two parts: compensation for inflation, plus the real return.

If i = 10% and π = 9%, the nominal return is 10% but the real return is 1%. Real activity responds to r, not i. The real rate is the price of spending today — the opportunity cost of what you forgo tomorrow — and it can go negative, which is policymakers pricing today's spending low enough to induce it.

This relation explains the Great Depression's worst mechanism. Nominal rates fell, but consumer prices collapsed, so inflation went sharply negative and the real interest rate soared. Borrowing became ruinously expensive exactly when the economy needed spending.

The moves
  1. To compare money across years, multiply by new CPI over old CPI. Sanity-check the direction.
  2. To judge whether something got more expensive in real terms, compare its price growth factor to the CPI's growth factor.
  3. Whenever interest rates appear, convert to real: r = i − π.
Traps
  • Flipping the CPI ratio. Converting an old amount to today's dollars must make it bigger.
  • Confusing the CPI's fixed quantities with real GDP's fixed prices.
  • Reasoning about spending with the nominal rate. Use the real rate.
Drills · Unit 18
examA Big Mac cost $1.60 in 1986 and $5.65 in 2021 — about 3.5× higher. The CPI went from about 110 to about 271 — about 2.5× higher. Has the real price risen, fallen or stayed flat?
Answer

The real price has increased. The Big Mac rose by a factor of 3.5 while the general price level rose by a factor of 2.5. Since the good outpaced the basket, it costs more in inflation-adjusted terms.

Put concretely: if your income grew at the rate of inflation, you can buy fewer Big Macs in 2021 than in 1986.

examFollow-up: can making and serving a Big Mac be automated? What does that imply for its marginal cost over time?
Answer

No — it requires workers and a physical facility. Cooking and serving are hard to move into a factory.

So the mechanism that drove down the real price of manufactured goods — factory automation cutting marginal costs — is largely unavailable here. Marginal cost stays tied to labor costs, which rise with wages, so the real price drifts up rather than down. Contrast the desktop computer, which fell from $7,664 in today's dollars to about $1,000.

calcCal in-state tuition was $1,296 in 1985–86 when the CPI was 108.94. The CPI is now 300.536. What is that in today's dollars, and what does it tell you given tuition is now about $14,796?
Answer
$1,296 × (300.536 / 108.94) = $3,575

So 1985 tuition was equivalent to about $3,575 today. Actual tuition is roughly $14,796 — more than four times as much.

Real tuition and fees have risen enormously, far faster than general inflation. This is not a price-level story; it is a real change in the cost of public higher education.

conceptWhy did the real interest rate rise during the early Great Depression even as nominal rates fell, and why did it matter?
Answer

Because r = i − π, and π went sharply negative. Consumer prices collapsed after 1929, so deflation was severe. Subtracting a large negative number from a small positive nominal rate produces a high real interest rate.

Why it mattered: real activity responds to the real rate. A soaring real rate made borrowing ruinously expensive and rewarded holding cash, choking off exactly the investment and consumption the economy needed. It also raised the real burden of existing debts. Monetary policy was effectively very tight at the worst possible moment, which is why the Depression deepened rather than self-correcting.
