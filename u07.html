Taxes & Subsidies

Classes 6–7 · Chapter 4 Midterm + Final

A tax drives a vertical wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep. Draw the wedge and everything else follows.

Collected from producers, a tax is an extra marginal cost, so supply shifts up by the tax. Collected from consumers, it is a reduction in marginal benefit, so demand shifts down by the tax. Here is the result that surprises people and shows up on exams: both produce exactly the same equilibrium — same quantity, same price paid by consumers, same price received by producers. Legal incidence does not determine economic incidence.

Who actually bears it

One sentence to memorize: inelastic factors bear the tax; elastic factors escape it.

  • The less elastic demand is, the more consumers bear.
  • The less elastic supply is, the more producers bear.
  • The more elastic either side is, the more quantity falls and the bigger the deadweight loss.

The empirical check: European countries tax gasoline far more heavily than the US, and European pump prices are correspondingly higher — meaning consumers are bearing it. That implies gasoline supply is far more elastic than demand, which makes sense: supply is the world oil price plus the cost of running a station, while short-run demand is a commute.

This gives the efficiency rule for tax policy: taxing inelastic goods is more efficient, because less quantity is distorted. Cigarettes are the classic example. That is an efficiency claim, not a fairness claim.

Where the competitive model breaks

Benzarti and coauthors (2020) studied VAT changes in Europe, including a natural experiment where Finnish hairdressers got a 14-point VAT cut in 2007 that was repealed in 2012, with beauty salons as a control. They found tax cuts are only about 50% passed through to consumers, while tax increases are passed through almost fully.

The likely explanation is that consumers are inattentive to taxes, so producers pocket some of a cut, while an increase is something firms can point to when justifying a price rise. Edwards's conclusion is worth quoting on a cheat sheet: price determination does not work like the basic competitive model, and we should not take its predictions as gospel.

Subsidies

A subsidy is a negative tax and it also creates deadweight loss — in the other direction. It pushes quantity above the competitive level, so at the subsidized quantity MB < MC. Consumers pay PC, producers receive PC + subsidy, and the government pays the rectangle, which is larger than the combined gain in consumer and producer surplus. The difference is the triangle.

The moves
  1. Draw the no-tax equilibrium, then draw the wedge of height = tax between the curves at the new quantity.
  2. Mark three prices: what buyers pay, what sellers keep, and the original price in between.
  3. Revenue is a rectangle — tax × Q₂. Deadweight loss is the triangle to its right.
  4. For incidence, say which side is more inelastic and conclude that side bears more.
Traps
  • Claiming the side that legally pays the tax bears it. Both diagrams give the same answer.
  • Assuming the price rises by the full amount of the tax. It rises by less, unless demand is perfectly inelastic.
  • Saying subsidies have no efficiency cost. They overproduce, and that is a real triangle.
  • Confusing revenue with deadweight loss. Revenue is a transfer; only the triangle is destroyed.
Drills · Unit 7
graphDraw the effect of a 50¢ per gallon gasoline tax collected from producers. Then show a tax collected from consumers instead. Compare.
Answer

From producers: supply shifts up by 50¢, since the tax is an extra marginal cost. New quantity Q₂, consumers pay P₂, producers keep P₂ − 0.50.

From consumers: demand shifts down by 50¢, since the tax is a negative marginal benefit. New quantity Q₂, producers receive P₂, consumers pay P₂ + 0.50.

The two are identical in substance: same quantity traded, same price paid by consumers, same price kept by producers. Only the drawing differs. Legal incidence is irrelevant to economic incidence.

conceptEuropean gas taxes are far higher than American ones, and European pump prices are correspondingly higher. What does that tell you about the elasticities?
Answer

Prices rising roughly with the tax means consumers bear most of it, which happens when demand is much less elastic than supply.

That fits the economics: supply is the world oil price plus the cost of running a station, which is very elastic, while short-run demand is a commute with no good substitute.

conceptFinnish hairdressers got a 14-point VAT cut in 2007, repealed in 2012. Pass-through was about 50% on the cut and nearly 100% on the increase. Why, and what is the lesson?
Answer

The likely mechanism: consumers are inattentive to taxes. When the tax falls, firms can quietly pocket half of it because customers are not tracking the statutory rate. When it rises, firms can justify a visible price increase by pointing at the tax, so they pass nearly all of it along.

The lesson is that price determination does not actually work like the basic competitive model — its symmetric predictions should not be taken as gospel. Beauty salons served as the control group in the study.

graphDraw a subsidy. Show consumer surplus, producer surplus, the government's cost, and the deadweight loss. Why is there a loss when everyone seems better off?
Answer

The subsidy drives a wedge the opposite way: quantity rises to Q₂ > Q₁, consumers pay PC below P₁, producers receive PC + subsidy above P₁.

Consumer and producer surplus both rise, but the government pays a rectangle bigger than their combined gain. The excess is the deadweight loss triangle, sitting to the right of Q₁ between supply and demand.

The intuition: on every unit past Q₁, MB < MC. Society spends more to make those units than anyone values them at. Taxpayers fund the gap.

conceptWhy is taxing cigarettes more efficient than taxing a good with elastic demand?
Answer

Because deadweight loss comes from distorted quantity, not from the tax itself. Cigarette demand is highly inelastic, so the tax barely changes how much is traded, and the triangle stays small while revenue stays large.

With elastic demand, the same tax collapses quantity and produces a big triangle for less revenue. Note this is purely an efficiency argument — it says nothing about whether such taxes are fair, and taxes on inelastic necessities tend to be regressive.
