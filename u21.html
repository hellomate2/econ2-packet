Fiscal Policy

Class 20 · Chapter 25 Final

Fiscal policy is tax rates, transfers and government purchases. Definitions first, because they are easy to invert:

  • Budget surplus = T − G.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy increases the surplus, which lowers PAE.
  • Expansionary fiscal policy decreases the surplus, which raises PAE.
  • Countercyclical means working against the business cycle — expansionary in recession.

Deficit versus debt: the deficit is the annual excess of spending over taxes; the debt is the accumulation of past deficits.

The composition point that gets tested

Look again at PAE = (C̄ − cT + IP + G + NX) + cY. G enters one-for-one; T enters multiplied by c. So:

$100bn more G ⟹ PAE rises $100bn
$100bn of tax cuts ⟹ PAE rises only c × $100bnBecause households save part of a tax cut. Dollar for dollar, spending is a stronger stimulus than tax cuts — though tax cuts may act faster.

The three design questions for any stimulus, from the 2009 Recovery Act: how big (needs an estimate of how far PAE shifted, which is not directly observable in real time), what composition (different instruments have different multipliers and speeds), and how long (needs a forecast of how long PAE stays depressed — if more than a year, the stimulus must last more than a year).

2008, as the worked case

The housing bubble had raised both IP and C above where they would otherwise have been, and left households heavily indebted. When house prices collapsed:

  • Investment fell — the housing bust cut the expected future MRPK of housing, which is a kind of capital, and the financial crisis damaged animal spirits and made credit hard to get.
  • Consumption fell — the housing and stock declines destroyed wealth, and the crisis hurt confidence and restricted household credit.

Both push PAE down, so Y falls by a multiplied amount.

Why the debt matters

Federal debt held by the public is historically high as a share of GDP, and the CBO projects the July 2025 reconciliation bill adds about 9.5 percentage points by 2034 — from roughly 118% to 127.5% of GDP in 2035. The concerns:

  • More debt probably raises interest rates.
  • Debt rising too fast as a share of GDP can trigger a debt crisis if investors lose confidence quickly.
  • Debt can be serviced rather than repaid, but that means future generations pay more to carry it.

The constraints are real: savers must be willing to buy the debt; state and local governments face balanced budget requirements that force service and school cuts in downturns; and the federal government faces debt ceilings and budget fights — the 2025 shutdown was the longest on record, ended by a continuing resolution on 12 November 2025. Edwards's normative line: debt should not cause paralysis, but in good times we should run surpluses and keep our powder dry.

The moves
  1. Identify which component the policy hits. G is direct; T works through c.
  2. Compute the change in autonomous spending, then multiply by 1/(1 − c).
  3. To fully offset a shock, match the shift in PAE, not the change in Y.
  4. Add the caveat about timing and magnitude — it is a real source of credit.
Traps
  • Treating a $100bn tax cut as equivalent to $100bn of spending. It is smaller by a factor of c.
  • Inverting the definitions — expansionary policy reduces the surplus.
  • Sizing the offset to the output gap rather than to the PAE shift.
Drills · Unit 21
calcWith mpc = 0.5, compare $100bn of extra government purchases with $100bn of tax cuts.
Answer

Spending: G enters autonomous spending one-for-one, so PAE rises $100bn and Y rises 100 × 1/(1−0.5) = $200bn.

Tax cut: taxes enter as −cT, so PAE rises only 0.5 × 100 = $50bn, and Y rises 50 × 2 = $100bn.

Dollar for dollar, spending is twice as powerful here, because households save part of a tax cut rather than spending it. The counterargument is speed: tax cuts can reach people faster than new spending programs can be stood up.

graphShow a fall in autonomous consumption followed by an offsetting tax cut on one Keynesian Cross.
Answer

Start at PAE₁ crossing the 45° line at Y*. The fall in C̄ shifts the line down in parallel to PAE₂, and output falls to Y₂ — by more than the shift, thanks to the multiplier.

The tax cut raises disposable income and therefore consumption, shifting the line back up to PAE₃. If sized correctly, PAE₃ coincides with PAE₁ and output returns to Y*.

The sentence that earns the last point: the keys to getting this right are timing and magnitude, and neither is obvious in real time — you cannot directly observe how far PAE has shifted.

conceptWhy did the housing bust reduce both investment and consumption?
Answer

Investment: housing is a form of capital, and the bust slashed its expected future MRPK, which by Unit 14 lowers the present value of new housing capital and therefore investment in it. The financial crisis then damaged animal spirits and made credit hard for firms to obtain.

Consumption: collapsing house and stock prices destroyed wealth, and wealth is a determinant of C. The crisis also hurt consumer confidence and restricted household access to credit.

Both shift PAE down, and the multiplier turns that into a larger fall in output.

conceptGive three reasons to worry about government debt.
Answer
  • Higher interest rates. More government borrowing probably pushes rates up, which crowds out private investment.
  • Debt crisis risk. If debt rises too far as a share of GDP, investors can lose confidence quickly and abruptly.
  • Intergenerational burden. Debt can be serviced rather than repaid, but servicing costs mean future generations pay more.

Context: federal debt held by the public is at historically high shares of GDP, and the CBO projects the July 2025 reconciliation bill raises it about 9.5 percentage points by 2034. Edwards's line is that debt should not cause paralysis, but in good times governments should run surpluses.
