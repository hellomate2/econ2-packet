Monopoly & Market Power

Class 10 · Chapter 8 Midterm + Final

Edwards flagged this one in lecture as likely to be on the exam, and it was — the Taylor Swift question on the midterm and a multiple choice on the final.

A monopolist faces the whole market demand curve, so to sell one more unit it must cut the price — and it must cut it on every unit it sells, not just the new one. That is why marginal revenue falls below price and keeps falling.

If P = α − βQ, then TR = αQ − βQ² and MR = α − 2βQSame intercept as demand, twice as steep. That is the fact you draw with.

The profit rule is unchanged — MR = MC. What changes is the reading:

The welfare accounting

Using the lettered regions from lecture: under competition consumer surplus is a+b+c+d+e and producer surplus is f+g+h. Under monopoly consumer surplus shrinks to a+b, producer surplus becomes c+d+f+g, and e+h is deadweight loss. Region c+d is the distributional story — it was consumer surplus under competition and becomes producer surplus under monopoly.

Three things Edwards stresses that students get wrong:

  • A monopolist has no supply curve. For a given demand curve there is exactly one quantity it wants to supply; it does not trace out a price-quantity relationship.
  • A monopolist does not produce where MC = P, and therefore not where MB = MC. That gap is the inefficiency.
  • Monopoly does not guarantee profit. If ATC is high enough at Qm, the monopolist can break even or lose money. But unlike a competitive firm it can sustain positive profit forever — by definition, since barriers to entry are what make it a monopoly.

Where market power comes from

Barriers to entry: high fixed costs (railways), patents and legal protections (pharmaceuticals), licensing and gatekeeping. A natural monopoly is where one firm can serve the whole market at lower average cost than several — utilities like PG&E, railways, and anything with near-zero marginal cost such as software and digital media. Network effects make Facebook and rideshare platforms behave similarly, since value rises with the user base.

The patent tradeoff

If monopoly is bad, why do we grant patents? Because innovation is costly and easy to copy. Drug development has huge fixed costs and low marginal costs; without protection, competitors reverse-engineer the product and the innovator never recoups. Patents buy innovation with a time-limited monopoly.

The evidence is genuinely mixed. It is hard to show that more generous patents produce more innovation; it is easy to show firms patent more strategically when protection expands; and there is evidence patents hinder follow-on innovation. Much long-run growth may come from innovations in the public domain.

Antitrust, then and now

The Gilded Age consolidated railroads, shipping and oil into monopolies — Standard Oil was broken up in 1911. Early-1900s antitrust dismantled them and utilities were regulated. Deregulation from the late 1970s let concentration return in cable, broadband and tech.

The modern debate turns on the consumer welfare standard, dominant since the late 1970s: if prices are low and consumers are not visibly harmed, concentration alone is not a problem. That standard let firms like Amazon, Google and Facebook grow by pricing low and acquiring potential competitors — Instagram and WhatsApp being the standard examples. The EU, and the US under Lina Khan's FTC, pushed back. Recent milestones: a 2024 judgment that Google holds an illegal monopoly in search, and a 2024 suit alleging Visa stifled debit-card competition.

The moves
  1. Draw D, then MR with the same intercept and twice the slope, then MC.
  2. Quantity from MR = MC. Price from the demand curve above it. Never read price off MR.
  3. Mark the competitive benchmark where MC crosses D, and put the deadweight loss triangle between the two quantities.
  4. If asked about profit, add ATC and use (P − ATC) × Q.
Traps
  • Reading the monopoly price off the MR curve. It is a very common and very costly error.
  • Drawing MR with the same slope as demand. Twice as steep.
  • Assuming monopolists always profit. It depends on ATC.
  • Saying the monopolist "restricts supply." It chooses a point on demand; it has no supply curve.
Drills · Unit 10
examCompared to perfect competition, a monopolist does what to price and quantity?
Answer

Raises price and reduces quantity. It sets MR = MC rather than P = MC, and since MR lies below demand, the quantity where they cross is to the left of the competitive quantity — and the price read off demand above it is higher.

examThe Eras Tour sold 10 million tickets and grossed $2 billion. Does Taylor Swift have market power? Draw the market, and say whether the efficient number of tickets was sold.
Answer

Average price = $2bn / 10m = $200.

She has market power, emphatically. With perfect product differentiation there is exactly one producer of a Taylor Swift concert. Some fans may see other artists as substitutes, but nobody else can supply the good itself.

The diagram: downward-sloping demand, MR below it and twice as steep, upward-sloping MC. Qm = 10m where MR = MC; P = $200 read up on demand.

Not efficient. Where demand meets MC — the competitive benchmark — about 15 million tickets would trade. Monopoly pricing cut that by a third. On those 5 million missing tickets, willingness to pay exceeded marginal cost. A lot of unfulfilled Swifties.

calcSame tour. Fixed costs are zero and MC rises linearly from 0 to $100 at 10 million tickets. Are profits zero? If not, what are they?
Answer

Edwards notes on the answer key that he got this wrong at first — it is a genuine trap.

With zero fixed costs, total cost is the area under the marginal cost curve, which here is a right triangle with base 10 million and height $100:

TC = ½ × 10m × $100 = $500 million

Total revenue = $200 × 10m = $2 billion. So:

Profit = $2bn − $0.5bn = $1.5 billion

The trap is treating total cost as the rectangle $100 × 10m = $1bn, which would give $1 billion. Cost is the area under MC, and MC is a line, so it is a triangle.

conceptWhat is a natural monopoly, and give three examples.
Answer

A situation where one producer can supply the entire market at lower average cost than several firms could — typically because fixed costs are enormous and marginal cost is very low, so average cost keeps falling over the whole relevant range.

Examples: utilities (PG&E for gas and electricity), railways, and software and digital media, where marginal cost is essentially zero. Platforms like Facebook and Uber behave similarly through network effects — the service gets more valuable as the user base grows.

conceptIf monopoly causes deadweight loss, why does the US grant pharmaceutical patents?
Answer

Because inventing is expensive and copying is cheap. Drug development carries huge fixed costs and low marginal costs, so a competitor who reverse-engineers the molecule can undercut the innovator, who then never recovers R&D. A time-limited patent monopoly lets future profits fund the invention.

The honest caveats: it is empirically hard to show that more generous patents produce more innovation; it is easy to show firms patent more strategically when protection widens; there is evidence patents impede follow-on innovation; and much long-run growth may come from public-domain ideas and publicly funded research.

conceptWhat is the consumer welfare standard, and how did it enable today's large tech firms?
Answer

It is the doctrine dominating US antitrust since the late 1970s: if prices stay low and consumers are not demonstrably harmed, industry concentration is not itself a problem.

That let a generation of firms build dominance by pricing low and buying potential competitors — Facebook acquiring Instagram and WhatsApp is the canonical case. Google and Facebook do not even charge users; they monetize advertisers, so a price-focused test barely registers them. Amazon squeezes suppliers rather than consumers.

The EU and the US under Lina Khan's FTC pushed back, producing the 2024 judgment that Google illegally monopolized search and the 2024 Visa debit-card suit.
