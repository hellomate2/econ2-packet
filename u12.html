Labor Markets & Wages

Class 12 · Chapters 13, 20 Final

The labor market inverts your usual role: you are the supplier, firms are the demanders, and the price is the wage.

Labor demand comes from profit maximization. A firm hires up to the point where one more worker's extra revenue equals the wage. That extra revenue is the marginal revenue product of labor:

MRPL = MPL × MR → hire until MRPL = WMPL is the extra output from one more worker; MR is the extra revenue per unit sold. For a competitive firm MR = P, and MPL × P is called the value of the marginal product.

MRPL slopes down for two reasons: MPL falls through diminishing returns — more workers without more capital or floor space means each adds less — and MR is constant or falling. Note carefully that the downward slope is not about market power.

Labor supply comes from utility maximization. Households value leisure as well as goods, the price of leisure is the wage, and the same rational spending rule applies. A wage rise creates a substitution effect (leisure is now expensive, work more) and an income effect (you are richer, take more leisure). Which dominates is an empirical question — the course assumes substitution wins, so labor supply slopes up.

One important complication on the technology row: rising productivity also shifts out the product supply curve and pushes the output price down. If that price fall is large enough, MRPL could in principle fall even as MPL rises. Historically this is not what happens — capital accumulation and technological progress have raised employment and wages, probably because product demand is elastic enough.

The Mariel Boatlift

Theory says a supply influx lowers wages. David Card tested it with a natural experiment: between May and September 1980, roughly 125,000 Cubans left Mariel for the US, and almost all went to Miami.

Why it is such a good design: earlier studies compared wages and immigrant counts across cities, but immigrant flows are not randomly assigned — immigrants often move to cities because labor demand is expanding, which can produce zero correlation even if the supply effect is exactly as theory predicts. The Marielitos left for political reasons, not because Miami's labor market was booming.

Card found no obvious effect on average earnings, and none on wage inequality among non-Cubans in Miami. His explanations: some migration to Miami that would otherwise have happened did not, and labor demand may have been very elastic — Miami had industries using low-skilled labor that could expand easily. Draw this as a nearly flat labor demand curve, where a big outward supply shift moves the wage barely at all.

The moves
  1. Decide whether the shock hits demand or supply. Output prices and productivity hit demand; the number of workers hits supply.
  2. If it hits demand, say which piece of MRPL = MPL × MR moved.
  3. Draw the shift, read off the new W and L, and name both.
  4. If the empirical result contradicts theory, reach for elasticity — a flat demand curve absorbs supply shifts with little wage movement.
Traps
  • Drawing immigration as a labor demand shift. Workers supply labor.
  • Saying MRPL slopes down because of market power. It is diminishing returns.
  • Claiming Card disproves supply and demand. He shows demand was elastic and the treatment was well-identified.
Drills · Unit 12
graphThe 2008 housing bubble bursts, cutting demand for construction services. Trace the effect through to construction workers' wages and employment, with both diagrams.
Answer

Diagram 1, market for construction services: demand shifts in. Price falls from P₁ to P₂, quantity falls.

Diagram 2, market for construction workers: since MRPL = MPL × MR and MR is that output price, the fall in P lowers MRPL at every level of employment. Labor demand shifts in.

Result: wages and employment both fall. FRED data on hourly-paid construction employment show exactly this collapse after 2008.

conceptWhy does a union-negotiated wage above equilibrium reduce employment?
Answer

The negotiated wage acts as a price floor in the labor market. Firms will not pay any worker more than the marginal revenue product of the last worker hired, so at the higher wage WN they move back up the labor demand curve and cut employment to LD.

Meanwhile more workers want jobs at WN, so quantity supplied rises to LS. The horizontal gap LS − LD is unemployment among autoworkers. Those who keep their jobs are better off; those who lose them are not.

examWhy is the Mariel Boatlift a better research design than comparing wages and immigrant counts across cities?
Answer

Because immigrant flows across cities are not randomly assigned. Immigrants often move to a city precisely because its labor demand is expanding — so labor demand and labor supply shift together, and you can find no correlation between immigration and wages even when the supply effect is exactly as theory predicts.

The boatlift breaks that: 125,000 Cubans arrived in Miami between May and September 1980 for political reasons, not because Miami's labor market was booming. That makes it a clean supply shock, with good wage and employment data before and after.

Card found no obvious effect on average earnings or on wage inequality among non-Cubans. His explanations were that some other migration to Miami did not occur, and that labor demand was very elastic because Miami's low-skill industries could expand easily.

conceptCould a productivity-raising technology ever lower the wages of the workers using it?
Answer

In principle yes. Higher productivity shifts out the product supply curve, which pushes the output price down. Since MRPL = MPL × MR and MR is that price, a large enough price fall could outweigh the rise in MPL and pull labor demand in.

But this is not the normal case. Historically capital accumulation and technological progress have raised employment and wages, probably because product demand is elastic enough that prices do not collapse. The tax-preparation example illustrates the normal case: MPL rose faster than prices fell, so MRPL and wages went up.
