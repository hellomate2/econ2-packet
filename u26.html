The Midterm, Worked

Fall 2025 · 60 points · 60 minutes Do this closed-book first
How to use this

60 minutes, 60 points. Do the whole thing closed-book on paper before opening any answer. Then grade yourself honestly.

16 pts short questions · 24 pts eggs · 18 pts Taylor Swift · 2 pts admin. Questions appear here in exam order, so you practise switching topics under time pressure — which is the actual skill being tested.

Section 1 · Short questions (16 pts)
6 ptsWhich curve slopes downward, and why? Then: lunches went $10 → $11, quantity 50,000 → 47,500 — elasticity? Then: the price of clothing falls; how do you restore the Rational Spending Rule?
Answers

(a) Demand. It slopes down because marginal benefit, marginal utility, or willingness to pay falls as quantity rises. More is better, but at a decreasing rate.

(b) +10% price, −5% quantity, so εD = −5/10 = −0.5. Relatively inelastic — smaller than 1 in magnitude.

(c) A lower Pc raises muc/Pc. Buy more clothing, which reduces muc through diminishing marginal utility until the ratios are equal again.

4 ptsBill and Chris: who specializes in what, and why? Then: a fishhook doubles both men's fishing productivity — what changes?
Answers

(d) Chris fishes, Bill gathers coconuts. Chris's opportunity cost of a coconut is 4 fish; Bill's is 1 fish. Equivalently Bill's opportunity cost of fishing is higher than Chris's.

(e) Nothing qualitatively. Bill: 2 fish or 1 coconut. Chris: 16 fish or 2 coconuts. A coconut now costs Bill 2 fish and Chris 8 fish — Chris still comparatively much better at fishing. Both are richer, and if they tire of fish Chris may fish somewhat less, but Bill always specializes in coconuts.

6 ptsMost important costs of attending UC Berkeley? Then: can there be an optimal level of polluting? Then: why is the area above supply and below price called producer surplus?
Answers

(f) Tuition and fees plus forgone earnings — with average earnings near $60,000, forgone earnings likely exceed tuition except at private colleges. Room, board and books are secondary. Full credit requires forgone earnings.

(g) Yes, and it is generally not zero. If a polluting activity raises social marginal cost by a quantifiable amount, the socially optimal quantity sits where SMC = SMB, typically nonzero and finite. It could be zero, but there is no reason to expect it must be.

(h) Producers to the left of equilibrium receive the market price while paying only marginal cost, and the area under MC is total variable cost. The gap on each unit is the surplus. MC is also the reservation price below which they would never sell.

Section 2 · Eggs (24 pts)
10 ptsShape of egg demand? Marginal cost of an egg once you own the farm? Is a California chicken farm a barrier to entry over 1–2 years, and over 5? What does all that imply about supply?
Answers

(a) Demand is inelastic — relatively vertical, at least short run — because there is no single 1:1 substitute. Cooks need eggs for many dishes, so willingness to pay is potentially very high.

(b) Marginal cost is low. It is the cost of keeping one more laying hen — feed, water, henhouse, some fencing or a guard dog. It still rises with production, but from a low base.

(c) A large fixed cost and a big short-run barrier. A California farm needs substantial land, and California land is expensive even inland. Over 5 years it is a much smaller barrier, essentially by definition — fixed costs are only fixed in the short run.

(d) Supply slopes upward, since marginal cost rises for most physical goods, but sits low.

6 ptsDraw the US egg market with $2 a dozen and 10 billion dozen traded. Then: were the 2023 and 2025 price spikes supply or demand shocks?
Answers

(e) Axes P ($/dozen) and Q (billions of dozens). Steep demand (inelastic, no substitutes), upward but relatively flat supply starting low (low marginal cost). They cross at P = $2, Q = 10 billion. Mark both coordinates explicitly.

(f) Supply shock. Wild birds migrate in fall and spring, spreading avian flu, and prices peaked around January — right after the fall migration. There is no plausible reason bakers would demand far more eggs in January 2023, then not again until January 2025.

8 ptsRedraw for early 2025. Then: why did Trader Joe's hold prices down while Denny's added an egg surcharge?
Answers

(g) Same diagram, then supply shifts sharply in as avian flu kills laying hens. Because demand is steep, the new equilibrium is a large price rise and a small quantity fall. Mark both equilibria.

(h) The answer is about how much of each firm's marginal cost curve eggs actually drive.

  • Trader Joe's sells vast quantities of products containing no eggs — beer, liquor, produce, packaged goods. The egg shock barely moves its overall marginal cost, so it can absorb the increase, hold prices below market and ration at one dozen per visit.
  • Denny's is a breakfast diner. Eggs are in most of what it sells, so the shock shifts its marginal cost curve up substantially and cannot feasibly be weathered.

Both feared accusations of price gouging; only one could afford to act on that fear.

Section 3 · Taylor Swift (18 pts)
8 pts10 million tickets, $2 billion gross. Average price? Is that high for three hours? How many Taylor Swifts are there? Is she in a perfectly competitive market?
Answers

(a) $2bn / 10m = $200.

(b) Reasoned answers get full credit. The benchmark: an average hourly wage near $36 makes three hours worth about $108, so $200 is high relative to the opportunity cost of time — though a concert is not the same good as three hours of anything else.

(c) One. Live shows especially are unique. That is perfect product differentiation and an absolute barrier to entering the market for Taylor Swift products.

(d) She has market power, emphatically. There is no other producer in the market for her concerts. Some demanders may see other artists as substitutes, but nobody else can produce the good.

10 ptsDraw the Eras Tour market with all curves needed. With zero fixed costs, are profits zero? Should more tickets have been sold?
Answers

(e) Downward-sloping demand; MR with the same intercept and twice the slope; upward-sloping MC as given. Qm = 10 million where MR = MC; P = $200 read up on demand. Mark the competitive point where MC meets demand.

(f) Profits are not zero. With zero fixed costs, total cost is the area under MC — a right triangle with base 10 million and height $100:

TC = ½ × 10m × $100 = $500m; TR = $200 × 10m = $2bn; π = $1.5 billion

Edwards notes he got this wrong at first by using the rectangle ($1bn). Full credit was given for $1 billion too — but the triangle is right.

(g) No — too few tickets. Efficiency requires demand = MC, at about 15 million tickets. Monopoly pricing at MR = MC cut that to 10 million, a reduction of one third. Profit maximization pushes the equilibrium below the point where willingness to pay equals marginal cost. A lot of unfulfilled Swifties.
