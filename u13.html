Inequality

Class 13 · Chapter 13 Final

There are three kinds — opportunity, wealth, and income inequality — and this course focuses on labor income inequality, because it is the main driver of rising overall income inequality in the US, an important source of rising wealth inequality, and the one thing you have a tool for: the labor supply and demand diagram.

The facts, from Piketty and Saez

Their method matters as much as their result. Survey data cannot see the top of the distribution, so they used published IRS income tax data going back to 1913, combined with total income and household counts, to compute the share of income going to the top 1%, top 10% and so on. A "fractile" is any slice defined by percentiles.

The shape: US top income shares were high before World War II, low through the 1970s, and rising strongly since. The very top — the 0.01% — rose fastest. And the driver of the recent rise is largely labor income, not capital income, which is why the labor market diagram is the right tool.

Explanation 1 — skill-biased technological change

Computers and related technologies raised the marginal product of highly educated workers, shifting high-skill labor demand out while leaving most low-skill labor demand where it was. High-skill wages rise, low-skill wages stagnate, the gap widens.

Explanation 2 — globalization

The US has a comparative advantage in goods using high-skilled labor and a comparative disadvantage in goods using low-skilled labor. Opening to trade means specializing in and exporting the former while importing the latter. The mechanism is prices: the price of high-skill-intensive goods rises and the price of low-skill-intensive goods falls. Through MRPL = MPL × MR, that raises high-skill labor demand and lowers low-skill labor demand — same widening, different cause.

Explanation 3 — institutions and power

Labor markets may not be competitive. Employers facing little hiring competition can pay below MRPL (monopsony — flagged as ECON 151 material and explicitly excluded from Edwards's exams). Private-sector union membership has fallen steeply, the real federal minimum wage has eroded, and social norms around pay have shifted.

The race between education and technology

Skill-biased technological change raises inequality; rising educational attainment lowers it by increasing the supply of skilled workers. Through the 1950s and 1960s the two roughly balanced. From around 1970, growth in educational attainment slowed, and technology pulled ahead. That framing is the cleanest one-sentence answer to "why did inequality rise?"

Why it matters, and what to do

Inequality and poverty correlate, both damage people and the economy, and inequality may erode social cohesion and democracy. Case and Deaton's work on rising mortality and morbidity among less-educated Americans is the human end of this.

Three families of remedy:

  • Education — and not only college. Heckman's evidence is that returns to human capital investment are highest at the earliest ages for disadvantaged children. Slow-acting but powerful.
  • Targeted help for displaced workers — Trade Adjustment Assistance (job training, search help, extended benefits, temporary wage subsidies), which Edwards notes is tiny and little used; and place-based policies that subsidize employers to locate in troubled localities.
  • Raising incomes at the bottom directly — minimum wage, the Earned Income Tax Credit, and direct cash transfers such as a universal basic income.
The moves
  1. Draw two labor markets side by side, low-skill and high-skill. Almost every inequality question is a shift in one panel.
  2. Route every mechanism through MRPL = MPL × MR. Technology moves MPL; trade moves MR.
  3. Name the data source when you can — Piketty and Saez for shares, Autor for wages by education, Card for immigration.
  4. For remedies, say which curve the policy shifts.
Traps
  • Treating inequality as all capital income. The recent US rise is largely labor income.
  • Using survey data to talk about the top 1%. It cannot see them — that is why tax records were needed.
  • Describing the EITC as raising the wage firms pay. It lowers the market wage and tops workers up.

graphUse two labor market diagrams to show how skill-biased technological change raises inequality.
Answer

Draw low-skill and high-skill markets side by side, each with its own supply and demand.

Computers and related technology raise MPL for highly educated workers, so high-skill labor demand shifts out: WH and LH both rise. Meanwhile low-skill labor demand barely moves, so WL is roughly unchanged.

The wage gap widens — which is what Autor's series on real wages of full-time male workers by education level shows.

conceptHow does globalization widen the skill wage gap, in MRPL terms?
Answer

The US has a comparative advantage in high-skill-intensive goods and a disadvantage in low-skill-intensive ones. Opening to trade means exporting the former and importing the latter, and the mechanism is prices: the price of high-skill-intensive output rises, the price of low-skill-intensive output falls.

Since MRPL = MPL × MR and MR is that output price, high-skill labor demand shifts out and low-skill labor demand shifts in. Same divergence as skill-biased technological change, but driven by MR instead of MPL.

conceptExplain "the race between education and technology."
Answer

Two opposing forces. Skill-biased technological change keeps raising demand for educated workers, which widens the wage gap. Rising educational attainment keeps raising the supply of educated workers, which narrows it.

Through the 1950s and 1960s the two roughly balanced, so inequality was stable. From around 1970, growth in educational attainment slowed, technology kept going, and the demand-side force won — which is when inequality began rising.

conceptWhy did Piketty and Saez use tax returns instead of surveys, and what did they find?
Answer

Surveys cannot see the top of the income distribution — too few very rich respondents, and underreporting. Tax records can, and US income tax data run back to 1913.

Their method: combine total income from other sources, household counts from the Census, and IRS tabulations of taxpayers by income bracket, to back out the share going to the top 1%, top 10% and finer fractiles.

The finding: top shares were high before WWII, low through the 1970s, and have risen strongly since — with the very top (the 0.01%) rising fastest, driven substantially by labor income.

graphShow the Earned Income Tax Credit in a labor market diagram. Who ends up capturing part of it?
Answer

The EITC is a wage subsidy that pays people to work, so it shifts labor supply out from S₁ to S₂.

Employment rises to L₂. The market wage firms pay falls to W₂, but workers receive W₂ + EITC, which is above the original W₁.

The incidence point: because the market wage falls, employers capture part of the credit. How much depends on the elasticity of labor demand — the flatter it is, the less of the subsidy leaks to firms.
