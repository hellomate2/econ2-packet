Surplus & Deadweight Loss

Class 5 · Chapters 5–6 Midterm + Final
If you remember one thing

Competitive equilibrium maximizes total economic surplus — and is blind to distribution. The deadweight loss triangle points toward the efficient allocation and opens outward toward the actual quantity.

The three areas

Consumer surplus
Area under demand, above the price. You valued the unit at your willingness to pay, paid the market price, keep the difference.
Producer surplus
Area above supply, below the price. You received the price, gave up the marginal cost, keep the difference.
Total economic surplus
Consumer + producer surplus = the area between MB and MC up to the quantity traded.

Allocative efficiency: three conditions at once

ConditionAt the competitive equilibrium
Right quantityProduced up to MB = MC
Right consumersGoes to those with the highest marginal benefit
Right producersMade by those with the lowest marginal cost

All three hold at once — the first welfare theorem, generalized by Arrow–Debreu. Efficiency says nothing about fairness: willingness to pay depends heavily on income, and surplus counts a dollar as a dollar no matter whose it is. Improving equity typically costs some surplus — the equity–efficiency tradeoff, and a legitimate thing to pay for.

Deadweight loss runs both ways

QuantityWhat goes wrongTypical cause
Too little (Q < Q₁)Units where MB > MC never happen — gains from trade left on the tableTax, price ceiling
Too much (Q > Q₁)Units where MC > MB — resources burned on things worth less than they costSubsidy

Rules that earn points

  1. Draw the efficient equilibrium first, even when the question is about a distortion. You need it as the reference point.
  2. The triangle spans efficient Q to actual Q, bounded above by demand and below by supply.
  3. Name every region you shade — consumer surplus, producer surplus, deadweight loss, tax revenue. Unlabelled shading earns nothing.
  4. Asked whether an outcome is efficient, answer with MB versus MC, not adjectives.
  5. A transfer is not a loss. Only the triangle disappears.
Traps
  • Thinking only underproduction causes DWL. Overproduction does too — that is how subsidies lose money.
  • Calling a transfer a loss. When surplus moves from consumers to producers or to the government, that is redistribution, not deadweight loss.
  • Claiming efficiency implies fairness. Say explicitly that surplus counts dollars regardless of who holds them.
Unit 4 quiz4 MCQ · 2 FRQ · ~12 min
MCQ 12 pts

Producer surplus is:

  • AThe area under the demand curve and above the market price
  • BThe area above the supply curve and below the market price
  • CTotal revenue, P × Q
  • DThe area under the supply curve up to the quantity sold
Answer

B — above supply, below the price.

On each unit the producer receives the price and gives up only the marginal cost; the gap is what is kept.

D is the trap: the area under the supply curve is total variable cost — the part producers do not keep.

MCQ 22 pts

Quantity traded is pushed above the competitive level. Why is there a deadweight loss?

  • AThere is none — more output always means more surplus
  • BOn each extra unit, marginal cost exceeds marginal benefit
  • COn each unit not traded, marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost
  • DProducers capture surplus that belonged to consumers
Answer

B — MC exceeds MB on the extra units.

Beyond Q₁ each unit costs more to make than it is worth to anyone, so producing it destroys value. This is what a subsidy does.

C is the trap: correct reasoning, wrong direction. That is the underproduction case.

MCQ 32 pts

A policy moves $50 million of surplus from producers to consumers and destroys a triangle worth $5 million. The deadweight loss is:

  • A$55 million
  • B$50 million
  • C$5 million
  • D$45 million
Answer

C — $5 million.

Deadweight loss is surplus that vanishes from the economy. The $50 million did not vanish; it changed hands.

B is the trap: producers really did lose $50 million, so it feels like a loss. From society's point of view it is redistribution.

MCQ 42 pts

Which statement about the first welfare theorem is correct?

  • ACompetitive equilibrium is both efficient and equitable
  • BCompetitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus but is blind to distribution
  • CTotal surplus is maximized only when incomes are equal
  • DIt guarantees the good goes to whoever needs it most
Answer

B — maximizes total surplus, blind to distribution.

Surplus counts a dollar as a dollar no matter who receives it, so an allocation can be perfectly efficient and deeply inequitable.

D is the trap: the theorem delivers the good to the highest willingness to pay, which depends on income — not to the greatest need.

FRQ 16 pts

Explain why the area above an upward-sloping supply curve and below the market price is called producer surplus. What are producers getting, and why is it a surplus?

Two short paragraphs. A small labeled sketch helps but is not required.
Model answer

For every unit to the left of equilibrium, producers receive the market price but give up only the marginal cost of producing that unit. The area under the marginal cost curve is exactly total variable cost, so the region between the price and the supply curve is what producers keep after covering those costs.

Marginal cost is also the reservation price below which a producer would never sell, so every unit sold above MC is a strict gain. Summing those gains across all units traded gives the triangle.

What earns the points
  • Saying the supply curve is the marginal cost curve.
  • Price received versus MC given up, stated unit by unit.
  • Identifying the area under supply as total variable cost.
  • The reservation-price argument for why the gain is strict.
FRQ 24 pts

Draw the deadweight loss when quantity is pushed above the competitive level, and explain in one sentence why it is a loss.

One diagram: axes P and Q, label S/MC and D/MB, mark Q₁ and Q₂, shade and label the triangle. Then one sentence.
Model answer

The triangle sits between the supply and demand curves from Q₁ out to Q₂, with its point at the efficient equilibrium and its open end at Q₂ — this time with supply above demand.

The sentence: on every unit beyond Q₁, the marginal cost of producing it exceeds the marginal benefit to anyone consuming it, so producing it destroys value. This is exactly what a subsidy does.

What earns the points
  • Efficient equilibrium drawn and labeled first.
  • Triangle pointing at Q₁ and opening toward Q₂, with supply above demand.
  • The shaded region labeled "deadweight loss."
  • The sentence stated as MC > MB, not as an adjective.
