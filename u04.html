Surplus & Deadweight Loss

Class 5 · Chapters 5–6 Midterm + Final

Now the same diagram is used to measure welfare — who gains how much.

  • Consumer surplus: area under the demand curve and above the price. You valued the unit at your willingness to pay, you paid the market price, you keep the difference.
  • Producer surplus: area above the supply curve and below the price. You received the price, you gave up the marginal cost, you keep the difference.
  • Total economic surplus = consumer + producer surplus = the area between MB and MC up to the quantity traded.

The first welfare theorem

Competitive equilibrium maximizes total economic surplus. At Q₁, three conditions hold at once: the good is produced up to MB = MC; it goes to the consumers with the highest marginal benefit; and it is produced by the producers with the lowest marginal cost. That is allocative efficiency, and the general version is the Arrow–Debreu first welfare theorem.

Now the part students skip and Edwards cares about: efficiency says nothing about fairness. Willingness to pay depends heavily on income, and surplus counts a dollar as a dollar no matter whose it is. The theorem is blind to distribution. Interfering to improve equity typically costs some efficiency — that is the equity–efficiency tradeoff, and it is a legitimate thing to want to pay for.

Deadweight loss

Deadweight loss is surplus destroyed by trading a quantity other than the efficient one. Crucially it runs both directions:

  • Too little (Q below equilibrium): units where MB > MC never happen. Gains from trade left on the table.
  • Too much (Q above equilibrium): units get produced where MC > MB. Resources burned on things worth less than they cost.

Edwards's practical rule, worth copying verbatim onto your cheat sheet: the deadweight loss triangle points toward the efficient allocation and opens outward toward the actual quantity. Get that and you can place the triangle in any diagram in this course.

The moves
  1. Draw the efficient equilibrium first, even when the question is about a distortion. You need it as the reference point.
  2. Mark the actual quantity. The triangle spans from efficient Q to actual Q, bounded above by demand and below by supply.
  3. Name every region you shade — "consumer surplus," "producer surplus," "deadweight loss," "tax revenue." Unlabelled shading earns nothing.
  4. If asked whether an outcome is efficient, answer with MB versus MC, not with adjectives.
Traps
  • Thinking only underproduction causes DWL. Overproduction does too — that is how subsidies lose money.
  • Calling a transfer a loss. When surplus moves from consumers to producers or to the government, that is redistribution, not deadweight loss. Only the triangle is lost.
  • Claiming efficiency implies fairness. Say explicitly that surplus counts dollars regardless of who holds them.
Drills · Unit 4
examExplain why the area above an upward-sloping supply curve and below the market price is called producer surplus. What are producers getting, and why is it a surplus?
Answer

For every unit to the left of the equilibrium, producers receive the market price but give up only the marginal cost of producing that unit — and the area under the marginal cost curve is exactly total variable cost. The gap between the price and marginal cost on each unit is what they keep.

For extra credit, add: marginal cost is the reservation price below which the producer would never sell, so every unit sold at a price above MC is a strict gain.

conceptState the three conditions for allocative efficiency.
Answer
  1. The good is produced up to the point where MB = MC.
  2. It is allocated to the consumers with the highest marginal benefit.
  3. It is produced by the producers with the lowest marginal cost.

Competitive equilibrium delivers all three, which is the first welfare theorem.

graphDraw the deadweight loss when quantity is pushed above the competitive level, and explain in one sentence why it is a loss.
Answer

The triangle sits between the supply and demand curves from Q₁ out to Q₂, with its point at the efficient equilibrium and its open end at Q₂ — this time with supply above demand.

The sentence: on every unit beyond Q₁, the marginal cost of producing it exceeds the marginal benefit to anyone consuming it, so producing it destroys value. This is exactly what a subsidy does.

conceptThe first welfare theorem says competitive markets are efficient. Give the strongest objection a Berkeley economist would raise.
Answer

It is blind to distribution. Consumer surplus is built from willingness to pay, and willingness to pay depends heavily on income. A dollar of surplus counts the same whether it goes to a rich or a poor household, so an allocation can be perfectly efficient and deeply inequitable at the same time.

Efficiency remains a worthy goal, but there is a genuine equity–efficiency tradeoff: interfering with prices to improve equity generally costs some surplus, and that may be worth paying.
