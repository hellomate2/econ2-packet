Monetary Policy & Financial Crises

Class 21 · Chapter 26 Final

The Federal Reserve is the US central bank, with a dual mandate: low, stable inflation and full employment. Its key policy lever is the real interest rate.

How the Fed moves rates. It is the only institution allowed to put dollars into circulation, which it does by creating money and trading it for financial assets — usually short-term government debt. These are open-market operations. Its two conventional tools are changing the interest rate paid on bank reserves and changing the supply of reserves. Note that this is not fiscal policy: the Fed is not spending or transferring, it is swapping assets.

How that reaches the real economy. The Fed sets a nominal rate i. Because of inflation inertia, π moves slowly, so by r = i − π changing i changes r in the short run. And from Unit 20, all three of C, I and NX fall when r rises. So:

Fed raises i ⟹ r rises ⟹ C, I, NX fall ⟹ PAE shifts down ⟹ Y falls (multiplied)Contractionary monetary policy. Expansionary runs the same chain in reverse. The rate-sensitive components are residential housing and new car purchases.

The crucial limit: the Fed controls r in the short run, not the long run. In the long run r must equal r*, the rate that equilibrates saving and investment. The Fed cannot hold r away from r* indefinitely — Unit 23 explains what stops it.

Three reasons a central bank acts

  • Countercyclical policy — offsetting a shock to PAE. The Fed cutting the funds rate through 2007–08 is the example.
  • Another objective — chiefly controlling inflation, which is Unit 23.
  • A mistake — monetary policy in the Great Depression, where the money stock and bank reserves contracted and the real interest rate soared, compounding the collapse in autonomous consumption. Two downward shifts of PAE, one of them self-inflicted.

This is why central bank independence matters. Erdoğan fired Turkey's central bank chief in March 2021 for raising rates, claiming that lowering rates would reduce inflation by cutting borrowing costs. Turkey hit 72% annual inflation in 2022.

Financial crises

Financial intermediation is the process of getting saving into productive investment — banks, investment banks, money market funds, pension funds. A financial crisis is when many institutions are in danger of failing, people lose confidence in them, and intermediation is widely disrupted.

How one institution fails. A bank's assets are loans, reserves and bonds; its liabilities are short-term borrowing and equity. If asset values fall below obligations, it is insolvent — and merely believing an institution may become insolvent can cause depositors to withdraw and lenders to stop lending, making the insolvency happen.

Silicon Valley Bank, 2023 is the course's worked example, and it is really a present value question. SVB held many long-term Treasury bonds. When interest rates rose, the present value of those fixed future payments fell, so the assets were worth far less than their book value. A bank run on 9 March, involving Founders Fund and other venture investors, depleted its cash — a bank lends out deposits rather than keeping them in a vault. SVB was allowed to fail, but the FDIC covered all deposits, beyond the $250,000 limit, and other banks acquired it cheaply.

Four contagion channels — worth memorizing as a list:

  1. Confidence: trouble at one institution creates doubt about others, even with no connection between them. First Republic's shares dived on contagion fear days after SVB.
  2. Linkage: direct exposure through loans, insurance contracts and counterparty relationships.
  3. Fire sale: a troubled institution dumps assets, driving down prices of assets everyone else holds.
  4. Macroeconomic: trouble reduces PAE and hence Y, which harms everyone.

A crisis hits PAE by raising credit spreads, tightening lending standards, and damaging confidence. Prevention: higher capital requirements, deposit insurance, regulating risk-taking and interlinkage, and keeping the macroeconomy stable.

When rates hit zero

Nominal rates cannot go much below zero, because currency offers a guaranteed zero nominal return. Hence unconventional tools: forward guidance, statements shaping expectations about future rates — the Fed's December 2008 and March 2009 language about "exceptionally low levels ... for an extended period" — and quantitative easing, buying bonds other than short-term government debt using reserves.

The moves
  1. Run the full chain explicitly: i → r (via inflation inertia) → C, I, NX → PAE → Y.
  2. Always say why changing i changes r: inflation moves slowly, so r = i − π shifts with i.
  3. For bank failures, reach for present value. Rates up ⇒ bond prices down ⇒ assets impaired.
  4. For crisis spread, name which of the four contagion channels applies.
Traps
  • Calling Fed actions government spending. Open-market operations are asset swaps.
  • Saying the Fed sets the real rate in the long run. It cannot — r must equal r*.
  • Forgetting inflation inertia as the reason the Fed has short-run traction at all.
Drills · Unit 22
conceptTrace the full chain from a Fed rate cut to higher GDP.
Answer
  1. The Fed lowers the nominal rate i via open-market operations and the rate paid on reserves.
  2. Because of inflation inertia, π barely moves in the short run, so by r = i − π the real rate falls too.
  3. A lower r raises IP (present values of future MRPK rise), raises C (saving is less attractive — think new car purchases), and raises NX (the dollar depreciates, Unit 24).
  4. PAE shifts up at every level of Y.
  5. Y rises by more than the shift, because of the multiplier.

Step 2 is the one students skip and it is the whole reason monetary policy works.

conceptWhy did Silicon Valley Bank fail, and which earlier concept explains it?
Answer

Present value — Unit 14. SVB held many long-term US Treasury bonds. When market interest rates rose, the present value of those fixed future payments fell:

Price = F₁/(1+i) + F₂/(1+i)² + … + FT/(1+i)T

The F's are fixed, so a higher i shrinks the price. SVB's assets were worth far less than book value.

Then the run: on 9 March 2023, Founders Fund and other venture investors pulled deposits en masse. A bank lends out its deposits rather than holding them in a vault, so it could not meet withdrawals. SVB failed, the FDIC covered all deposits beyond the $250,000 limit, and other banks acquired it cheaply.

conceptName the four channels by which a crisis spreads between institutions.
Answer
  1. Confidence — trouble at one institution creates doubts about others even with no connection. First Republic's shares dived on contagion fear right after SVB.
  2. Linkage — direct exposure through loans, insurance contracts and counterparty relationships.
  3. Fire sale — a troubled institution sells assets to raise cash, pushing down prices of assets other institutions hold, impairing them too.
  4. Macroeconomic — the trouble reduces PAE and hence Y, which harms every institution's borrowers.
conceptWhy does central bank independence matter? Give the recent example.
Answer

Because political incentives favor low rates in the short run, while price stability requires a willingness to raise them and accept a recession — Unit 23's mechanism.

Turkey. In March 2021 Erdoğan fired the central bank chief for raising rates, asserting that lowering rates would reduce inflation by cutting corporate borrowing costs. That contradicts the standard view that low rates push prices up by spurring activity. Turkey reached 72% annual inflation in 2022.

The Great Depression is the other side of the coin — not capture, but error: monetary contraction raised the real rate and deepened the collapse.

conceptWhat are forward guidance and quantitative easing, and why are they needed?
Answer

They are unconventional tools, needed because nominal interest rates cannot go much below zero — currency always offers a guaranteed zero nominal return, so nobody accepts meaningfully negative rates.

  • Forward guidance: statements or actions shaping expectations about future nominal rates. The Fed in December 2008 said weak conditions were "likely to warrant exceptionally low levels of the federal funds rate for some time," and in March 2009 upgraded that to "for an extended period."
  • Quantitative easing: buying bonds other than short-term government debt using reserves, to push down longer-term and riskier rates directly.
