Households & Utility

Classes 7–8 · Chapter 5 Midterm + Final

This unit derives where demand comes from. Households face a budget constraint and have preferences, and demand falls out of the optimization.

Pf·qf + Pc·qc = IncomeIntercepts: Income/P on each axis. Slope with clothing on the horizontal axis = −Pc/Pf.

A rise in income shifts the constraint outward in parallel. A rise in one good's price pivots it around the other good's intercept — steeper or flatter, same starting point.

Marginal utility and the rational spending rule

Total utility rises with quantity but at a decreasing rate; marginal utility is the slope of total utility, and it declines. Different goods have differently-shaped marginal utility curves.

The optimum is where the marginal utility of the last dollar spent is the same across all goods. Divide marginal utility by price to get marginal utility per dollar:

mua/Pa = mub/Pb = … = muz/PzThe Rational Spending Rule. If one ratio is higher, shift spending toward that good until declining marginal utility restores equality.

The comparative static they ask for

Suppose Pc rises 10%. If you do not change your purchases, the left side falls and the rule breaks:

muc/(1.1·Pc) < muf/Pf

To restore equality you buy less clothing, which raises muc (declining marginal utility running backwards), and possibly more food, which lowers muf. With numbers: if a garment gives 10 utils at $50 and food gives 2 utils at $10, both ratios are 0.2. If clothing goes to $55, its ratio falls to 10/55 ≈ 0.18. Cut clothing until muc reaches 11, and 11/55 = 0.2 again. Or split the adjustment across both goods.

Underneath sit two forces, which is an ECON 100A preview but does appear as a concept question:

  • Substitution effect: the good is now relatively expensive, so buy something else instead.
  • Income effect: your real purchasing power fell, so you can afford less of everything.
The moves
  1. Write the rule before doing anything: mux/Px = muy/Py.
  2. Say which side of the equation the shock hits and which way it moves.
  3. State the quantity adjustment and the marginal utility it triggers — "buy less clothing, which raises muc."
  4. For the graph: income shifts parallel, price pivots.
Traps
  • Equating marginal utilities instead of marginal utilities per dollar.
  • Shifting the whole budget line when only one price changed. Pivot it.
  • Saying "buy less clothing" and stopping. The mechanism is that buying less raises its marginal utility, which restores the equality.
Drills · Unit 8
examUnder the Rational Spending Rule with clothing and food, suppose the price of clothing falls. How could you change your purchases to restore equality?
Answer

If Pc falls, the left-hand side muc/Pc increases, because the denominator shrank. Clothing now delivers more utility per dollar than food.

To restore equality, increase your purchases of clothing. Buying more clothing reduces muc through diminishing marginal utility, pulling the ratio back down until it matches muf/Pf again.

calcCoffee at Strada costs $4; frozen yogurt at Yogurt Park costs $6. muc(1) = 10, muc(2) = 8, muf(1) = 12, muf(2) = 8. What is the optimal daily bundle?
Answer

Compute marginal utility per dollar for each unit:

  • Coffee #1: 10/4 = 2.5 · Coffee #2: 8/4 = 2.0
  • Yogurt #1: 12/6 = 2.0 · Yogurt #2: 8/6 ≈ 1.33

The rule is satisfied at 2 coffees and 1 frozen yogurt, where 8/$4 = 12/$6 = 2.0. Both margins deliver the same utility per dollar, and no reallocation improves things.

graphWeekly income is $100, food is $20, clothing is $50. Draw the budget constraint with intercepts and slope. Then show a rise in the price of clothing and a rise in income.
Answer

Food intercept: 100/20 = 5 meals. Clothing intercept: 100/50 = 2 garments. With clothing on the horizontal axis, slope = −Pc/Pf = −50/20 = −2.5 — one garment costs 2.5 meals.

Price of clothing rises: the line pivots clockwise around the unchanged food intercept, pulling the clothing intercept inward.

Income rises: the line shifts outward in parallel, since relative prices and therefore the slope are unchanged.

conceptDistinguish the substitution effect from the income effect when a price rises.
Answer
  • Substitution effect: the good is now relatively more expensive, so the household wants less of it and more of others. "If the price goes up, I'll buy something else."
  • Income effect: the household's real purchasing power has fallen, so it must spend less overall. "If the price goes up, I can't buy as much."

They can push the same way or opposite ways. Full treatment with indifference curves is ECON 100A.
