Capital, Interest & Present Value

Class 14 · Chapter 21 Final

The final exam spent a whole question on this, so know it cold.

In economics, capital means productive physical assets — machines, buildings, trucks, computers, infrastructure. The final's multiple choice tests exactly this: not financial assets, not accumulated savings. Physical capital is the stuff; financial capital is the funds used to buy it. And investment is the flow of new capital into the capital stock, which is also a component of GDP.

Renting capital works just like hiring labor:

MRPK = MPK × MRRent machines until the marginal revenue product of the last machine equals the rental price. The demand curve for capital slopes down for the same diminishing-returns reason as labor.

Present value — the engine of the whole unit

Capital is durable: you buy it today and it produces revenue for years. People prefer things today to things tomorrow, which is time preference, and that is why interest exists. To compare future dollars with today's dollars, discount them.

PV = F / (1 + i)tOne payment F received t years from now, at interest rate i.
PV = F₁/(1+i) + F₂/(1+i)² + F₃/(1+i)³ + …A stream of payments. This is the price of a bond, and with Ft = MRPK(t) it is the price of a machine.

Worked: $100 one year out at 3% is 100/1.03 = $97.09. At 8% it is 100/1.08 = $92.59. Two years out at 3% it is 100/1.03² = $94.26. And $1,000 a year for three years at 3% is 970.87 + 942.60 + 915.14 ≈ $2,827.83.

The two comparative statics that get tested:

  • Interest rate up ⇒ present value down. The denominators grow. This is why bond prices fall when rates rise — and why Silicon Valley Bank, holding long-term Treasuries, became insolvent in 2023.
  • Expected future payoffs down ⇒ present value down. If capital's future marginal product is expected to fall — say because less labor will be available to work alongside it — the price of capital falls today.

Firms buy machines until PV(stream of MRPK) = the purchase price. Raise the interest rate and PV falls, so firms buy fewer machines, which raises MRPK on the remaining ones until the equality is restored. That traces out the investment demand curve.

Stocks and bonds

Firms raise financial capital two ways: issue bonds (borrow, repay with interest) or issue stock (sell a share of future profits, paid out as dividends). And the same formula prices equity:

Stock price = PV(stream of expected future dividends)

So stock prices move for exactly two reasons. Interest rates: lower rates raise present values, and also signal expectations about economic activity. Expected future dividends: anything that changes them moves the price — for one firm or for all of them. The pandemic is the clean natural experiment: Zoom's expected dividends jumped while Carnival Cruise's collapsed, and their stock prices did the same.

The moves
  1. Write the PV formula before computing anything.
  2. For direction questions, ask which way the denominator or the numerator moved. That is the whole answer.
  3. If i = 0, every denominator is 1 and PV is just the sum of the payments. This exact question was on the final.
  4. For asset prices, say "price = present value of expected future payments," then name which input moved.
Traps
  • Defining capital as money. In economics it is physical productive assets.
  • Forgetting to raise (1+i) to the power t.
  • Saying rising rates make bonds more attractive so prices rise. Existing bonds have fixed payments, so their prices fall.
Drills · Unit 14
examIn economics, what does "capital" mean? (a) productive physical assets like equipment and structures, (b) financial assets like a bank account, (c) accumulated savings investable across borders, (d) all of the above.
Answer

(a) Productive physical assets like durable equipment and structures.

The distinction the course insists on: physical capital is the machines, buildings, trucks, computers and infrastructure; financial capital is the funds used to purchase, rent or build them. Human capital — education and training — also counts as capital in the growth chapters.

calcA bond pays F₁, F₂, F₃ over three years. (a) The interest rate rises — what happens to its price? (b) Express the price if i = 0.
Answer

(a) The price falls. The payments Ft are fixed, so raising i increases every denominator in PV = F₁/(1+i) + F₂/(1+i)² + F₃/(1+i)³, and the whole right-hand side shrinks.

(b) If i = 0 then 1 + i = 1 and every denominator is 1:

P = PV = F₁ + F₂ + F₃
examWhat happens to the price of a unit of capital when its future marginal product falls — for example, because less labor is expected to be available?
Answer

The price falls. A unit of capital is worth the present value of its future marginal revenue products, MRPK(t) = MR(t) × MPK(t).

If we expect less labor to work alongside the capital in future, its future marginal product falls, which shrinks every numerator in the present value sum and pulls down the price today. Machines are only valuable in proportion to what they will produce.

calcPresent value of $1,000 in each of the next three years at 3%?
Answer
1000/1.03 + 1000/1.03² + 1000/1.03³

= 970.87 + 942.60 + 915.14 = $2,827.83

Sanity check: it must be below $3,000 and not far below, since three years at 3% is modest discounting.

conceptWhy did Zoom and Carnival Cruise stock prices move in opposite directions in 2020?
Answer

Because a stock price is the present value of expected future dividends, and the pandemic revised those expectations in opposite directions — up for remote-work software, down for cruise operators.

It is a clean illustration that stock prices move for exactly two reasons: a change in the interest rate used to discount, or a change in expected future dividends, for one firm or for firms in general.
