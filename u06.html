Elasticity

Class 6 · Chapter 4 Midterm

Elasticity is the responsiveness of quantity to price, expressed as a ratio of percentage changes. Economists like it because it is unit-free — it does not care whether you measure in gallons or litres, dollars or yen.

εD = %ΔQD / %ΔP = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P)Interpretation: if price changes by 1%, quantity demanded changes by εD percent. Demand elasticities are negative; the course often compares |ε|.
ValueNameCurve looks
|ε| > 1ElasticRelatively flat
|ε| < 1InelasticRelatively steep
ε = 0Perfectly inelasticVertical
|ε| = ∞Perfectly elasticHorizontal

Elasticity is not slope. They are related but not the same: εD = (1/slope) × (P/Q). Because P/Q changes as you move along a straight line, elasticity varies along a linear demand curve even though the slope is constant. (Elasticity is constant only if log-log demand is a straight line.) "Steep means inelastic, flat means elastic" is a useful, crude approximation — and that is how Edwards describes it.

What makes demand elastic

  • Close substitutes make demand elastic; no substitutes make it inelastic. The midterm's egg question turns on this: there is no single 1:1 substitute for eggs, so demand is inelastic and relatively steep, especially in the short run.
  • Time. Gasoline demand is inelastic in the short run — you still have to commute — and elastic in the long run, once you can buy an electric car or move closer to work.
  • Necessity versus luxury. Things you can do without are elastic.

Supply is inelastic when there is a hard resource constraint, usually in the short run — drilling new oil wells or growing more soybeans takes time — and elastic in the long run as new technology and capacity arrive.

Elasticity and total expenditure

Total expenditure is P × Q, so what happens when price rises depends entirely on how hard quantity falls.

CasePrice rises 10%Expenditure
ε = −0.75 (inelastic)Q falls 7.5%: 200 → 18520,000 → 20,350 ▲
ε = −1.50 (elastic)Q falls 15%: 200 → 17020,000 → 18,700 ▼

So: inelastic demand means expenditure rises when supply contracts; elastic demand means it falls. The textbook application is a drug crackdown — if demand for an illicit drug is inelastic, raising its price increases total spending on it. The caveat Edwards adds: at high enough prices demand always becomes elastic, because budgets are finite.

The moves
  1. Compute percentage changes first, separately, then divide. Do not try to do it in one step.
  2. Report the sign and the magnitude, then classify: "−0.5, so relatively inelastic — smaller than 1 in magnitude."
  3. Justify elasticity with substitutes and time horizon, never with "people need it" alone.
  4. For expenditure questions, just compute new P × new Q. It is faster than reasoning verbally.
Traps
  • Saying elasticity equals slope. State that it is inversely proportional to slope, scaled by P/Q.
  • Claiming a linear demand curve has constant elasticity. It does not.
  • Dividing the wrong way round. Quantity change goes on top.
Drills · Unit 6
examLunches around Berkeley went from $10 to $11, and quantity demanded fell from 50,000 to 47,500. What is the price elasticity of demand? Elastic or inelastic?
Answer

Price rose 10%. Quantity fell by 2,500 from 50,000, which is 5%.

εD = −5% / 10% = −0.5

Relatively inelastic, because the magnitude is smaller than 1. Students around campus do not have great substitutes for lunch in the short run.

examThere is no single substitute for eggs — you can bake an eggless cake and scramble tofu, but the substitute depends on the dish. What does this imply about the shape of the market demand curve for eggs?
Answer

With no obvious 1:1 substitute, demand for eggs is inelastic — relatively steep, closer to vertical, at least in the short run. Cooks and bakers need eggs for many dishes and will keep paying as prices climb, so their willingness to pay is potentially very high.

This is what makes an avian-flu supply shock show up as a huge price spike rather than a big quantity drop.

calcDemand has ε = −0.75. Price rises from 100 to 110 and quantity falls from 200 to 185. What happens to total expenditure, and why?
Answer

Before: 100 × 200 = 20,000. After: 110 × 185 = 20,350. Expenditure rises by 1.75%.

Because demand is inelastic, the 7.5% quantity decline is smaller than the 10% price increase, so the price effect dominates. With ε = −1.5 instead, quantity would fall 15% to 170 and expenditure would drop to 18,700.

conceptWhy is gasoline demand inelastic in the short run but elastic in the long run?
Answer

In the short run you still have to get to work, you own the car you own, and you live where you live — so quantity barely responds to price.

In the long run the adjustment margins open up: buy an electric or more efficient car, move closer to work, change jobs, switch to transit. More substitution possibilities means more elastic demand. Elasticity generally rises with the time horizon, on both sides of the market.
